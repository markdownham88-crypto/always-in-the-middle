Always in the Middle

Process Change as Rhizomatic Assemblage — a disputation with the wheel, a counter-design in eight plateaus, and a practice for governance, risk and compliance

Principles don’t endure — they differ. Practice is how they repeat.

The Argument in Brief

In January 2015 a governance, risk and compliance practice set down a Process Change Methodology in seven stages — Discover, Define and Design, Review, Process Mapping, Prototype and Test, Business Acceptance, Embedding — closed by a band of Continuous Improvement and laid out as a wheel. Ten years later it was reissued under the banner Still Relevant, with the motto Principles endure. Practice evolves. This book takes the claim seriously enough to dispute it.

The dispute is not that the wheel is wrong. Within its proper domain — the ordered world, where cause and effect can be known in advance or discovered by analysis — it is a disciplined and humane instrument, better than most. The dispute is that the wheel presents itself as a theory of change as such, and that in doing so it carries an image of the organisation, of knowledge, of authority and of error that complexity science has spent half a century taking apart. A cycle is still a line; it has only been bent round until its two ends meet. It still has a first step and a last. It still has a centre, around which everything turns. It still treats what escapes the design as an exception to be brought back into line.

Against the wheel this book sets a different figure, drawn from Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari's A Thousand Plateaus (1980): the rhizome, a system without a root, without a centre, without a privileged beginning, in which any point can be connected to any other, and which — when it is broken at one place — starts up again along an old line or a new one. It sets beside it a second concept from the same authors, refined by Manuel DeLanda: the assemblage (agencement), a whole made of heterogeneous parts — people, rules, machines, documents, money, moods — whose properties emerge from their interaction and which can be taken apart and recomposed without the parts losing their identity.

From these two concepts, and from the wider body of complexity thinking — Prigogine and Stengers on far-from-equilibrium order, Kauffman on the adjacent possible, Cilliers on complexity and postmodernism, Stacey, Griffin, Shaw and Mowles on complex responsive processes of relating, Snowden on Cynefin and safe-to-fail probing, Weick on sensemaking, Hollnagel on work-as-done, Vaughan on the normalisation of deviance, Power on the audit society, Scott on legibility and mētis, Jullien on shì, the propensity of things — the book builds three things.

  1. A disputation (Part I), which examines the wheel stage by stage, names the assumption each stage takes for granted, and sets against it what complexity knows.
  2. An assemblage (Part II), in which the seven stages and their coda are transposed into eight plateaus — Enter in the middle; Compose the assemblage; Map, don't trace; Read the lines; Experiment in parallel; Enunciate together; Territorialise lightly; Follow the lines of flight — with no centre but a title that is itself only one more node, Version n − 1.
  3. A practice (Part III), which shows how a GRC function, a lender, a receiver or a regulator can actually work the assemblage: by sorting what it governs into three kinds of line — molar lines held rigid, molecular lines kept supple, lines of flight followed rather than suppressed — and by keeping an evidence trail that the board, the auditor and the supervisor can still read.

The thesis can be stated in a sentence. GOVERN THE LINES, NOT THE STAGES. Everything else is commentary — and since a rhizome has no last word, the commentary is the thing.

How to Read This Book

Deleuze and Guattari open A Thousand Plateaus by telling their reader that the plateaus may be read in any order, except the conclusion, which should be read last. A book about the rhizome that insisted on being read from front to back would be a performative contradiction — the content saying enter anywhere, the form saying begin at the beginning. This book therefore has three ways in.

The sequential way. The chapters are numbered and the argument does build. A reader who wants the case laid out as a case — critique, then construction, then practice — can read straight through. Nothing is lost; the line is a perfectly respectable form of the rhizome, one of its many possible paths.

The diagrammatic way. Each of the three Parts is anchored in one of the three diagrams of the counter-design: From Tree to Rhizome anchors Part I, The Assemblage anchors Part II, Working the Assemblage anchors Part III. A reader who begins with the diagram that most provokes them, and follows the chapters it calls up, will be reading as the diagrams were drawn.

The rhizomatic way. Every chapter ends with a panel headed Connects to, which lists other chapters with which it has an affinity — not a logical dependency, but a resonance. Follow those panels and the book becomes a network rather than a sequence. Some readers will find themselves in Chapter 13 (on how the rhizome fails) before they have read Chapter 5 (on what it is). That is not an error. It is the method.

Three kinds of apparatus run through the text. Scholia are marginal excursions that deepen a point without interrupting the argument; they can be skipped and returned to. Aphorisms, set in capitals, compress an argument into a sentence that can be carried away. Figures reproduce and annotate the three diagrams and several supporting schemata; each can be expanded. A glossary at the back defines the specialist terms — Deleuzian, complexity-scientific, and regulatory — and a bibliography lists the works drawn on.

A word on register. This is a work of philosophy applied to an institutional practice. It does not pretend that Deleuze wrote for compliance officers, nor that a chief risk officer needs to read Difference and Repetition to do her job. It claims something narrower and more useful: that the concepts Deleuze and Guattari built to think multiplicity, and the concepts complexity science built to think emergence, describe what governance professionals already encounter every day — the workaround that works better than the procedure, the near miss nobody logged, the control that is passed and the risk that is not, the policy that becomes true only when people make it so. The vocabulary is strange. The phenomena are not.

Prologue — The Wheel at Ten

There is a particular kind of document that every regulated organisation produces and almost none reads twice: the methodology. It arrives as an infographic, usually circular, usually coloured in a reassuring gradient, usually numbered. It promises that change, which is frightening, can be made orderly; that knowledge, which is scattered, can be gathered; that authority, which is contested, can be settled by a signature; and that error, which is endless, can be reduced to an exception.

The wheel of 2015 is a good example of the genre, and the reissue of 2025 an unusually honest one. Ten years on, and still relevant, it says — and then, as a strap-line, Principles endure. Practice evolves. At the centre of the wheel sit four words: Disciplined. Collaborative. Adaptive. Effective. Around the rim run seven stages, each with a short gloss. Discover and explore — engage, observe and understand the current state and requirements. Define and design — define operational and regulatory needs and design the right process solution. Review — challenge assumptions; consider impact and knock-on effects. Process mapping — map the process; walk through; validate what is really happening. Prototype and test — test in practice; gather feedback; revalidate and refine. Business acceptance — agree through governance and owner sign-off. Embedding — embed, monitor and enable; errors become exceptions. And beneath the wheel, as a bar running the full width of the page, a post-embedding stage of continuous improvement: test, assure, feedback, remediate, assess materiality, evolve techniques. In the background, faint, stands a tree.

That tree is where this book begins. It is a decorative flourish, almost certainly unconsidered; it was put there because trees connote growth, rootedness, endurance. But in the philosophy that this book draws upon, the tree is the master-image of a whole way of thinking — the way that proceeds from a root, through a trunk, out into branches; that organises knowledge as genealogy and authority as hierarchy; that always knows where the centre is and which way is up. Deleuze and Guattari call it arborescence, and they call it, bluntly, the image of thought that has dominated the West. The designer who put a tree behind the wheel was telling the truth about the methodology without meaning to. The wheel is a tree laid flat.

Look more closely and the wheel tells a second truth about itself. Its segments are numbered one to seven, but read clockwise around the rim they do not run one to seven. They run 1, 2, 3, then 7, 6, 5, 4. Process Mapping, the fourth stage, sits at nine o'clock, after Prototype and Test at seven o'clock and before Discover at eleven. Whether this was a design accident, a compromise with the layout, or a quiet acknowledgement that practice does not go round in order, the diagram cannot keep its own sequence. The wheel leaks. It knows — in the unconscious way that drawings know things — that the work does not proceed one stage after another; that mapping happens while testing, that review happens after embedding, that discovery happens all the time.

THE DIAGRAM ALREADY KNOWS WHAT THE METHODOLOGY DENIES.

This book takes that leak and widens it into a door. It asks what a methodology of change would look like if it began from the leak rather than from the wheel — from the fact that organisations are not trees but tangles, not sequences but simultaneities, not machines that can be redesigned but ecologies that are always already redesigning themselves. It asks what it would mean to govern such a thing: not to abandon governance, which would be both irresponsible and illegal, but to govern it in a way that fits what it is.

The answer it proposes is not a new wheel. A new wheel would be the same error with different labels. The answer is a figure with no centre and no rim, in which the seven stages survive — every one of them names something real that must be done — but survive as plateaus rather than steps: regions of intensity that can be entered from anywhere, that connect to every other, that recur rather than follow, and that are held together not by a sequence but by the lines that run through them all.

The headline of the counter-design is a deliberate reply to the original. Not Ten years on, and still relevant, but Ten years on, still in the middle. Deleuze and Guattari say that a rhizome has no beginning or end; it is always in the middle, between things, intermezzo. That is where every governance professional actually works — in the middle of a change programme that started before they arrived and will continue after they leave, in the middle of a regulatory regime that is being rewritten as they comply with it, in the middle of a set of relationships that no process map has ever captured. The wheel pretends that you can stand at its first segment and begin. The rhizome admits that you are already in it.

The motto is answered too. Not Principles endure; practice evolves, but Principles don't endure — they differ. Practice is how they repeat. This is the hinge of Deleuze's Difference and Repetition (1968), and it is the hinge of this book. A principle that endured unchanged through ten years of regulatory upheaval, technological transformation and a pandemic would not be a principle; it would be a fossil. What actually endures is something that repeats — and in repeating, differs. The disciplined practitioner does not apply the same principle to new circumstances; she produces the principle anew each time, and it comes out different because the circumstances made it so. That is not a failure of discipline. It is what discipline is when the world is complex.

Finally, the four central words. Disciplined, collaborative, adaptive, effective are virtues of a practitioner. The counter-design replaces them with the four principles of the rhizome itself: connective, heterogeneous, multiple, ruptural. These are not virtues one cultivates but properties of the thing one is working in. The shift is the shift of the whole book — from a methodology that describes what the change agent should be, to a cartography that describes what the change is.

The chapters that follow develop that cartography in full.

1The Wheel Examined: Anatomy of a Methodology

Before a thing can be disputed it must be understood, and understood generously. A disputation that sets up a straw wheel and knocks it down teaches nothing. This chapter therefore begins by doing the wheel the courtesy of reading it closely — its lineage, its logic, its virtues — before turning, in the chapters that follow, to what it cannot see.

1.1 A family of wheels

The Process Change Methodology of 2015 belongs to a large and distinguished family. Its oldest ancestor is the cycle Walter Shewhart drew at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the 1920s and 1930s — specification, production, inspection — which W. Edwards Deming carried to post-war Japan and which became, in the hands of his Japanese audiences, the Plan–Do–Check–Act cycle. Deming himself came to prefer Study to Check, and the distinction matters: to check is to compare a result with an expectation; to study is to learn something one did not expect. That small revision already contains, in germ, most of the argument of this book. The wheel was born with a question inside it about whether it is an instrument of control or an instrument of learning.

From Shewhart and Deming descend most of the cycles that govern organisational change today. Six Sigma's DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control — is the cycle hardened into a statistical discipline. Lean's kaizen cycles are the cycle shrunk to the scale of a workstation and repeated thousands of times. ITIL's continual service improvement is the cycle wrapped around an IT estate. Business process re-engineering, which Michael Hammer and James Champy made famous in the early 1990s with the injunction not to automate but to obliterate, was in one sense the cycle's revolutionary wing: instead of turning the wheel a notch, tear it off and fit a new one. And the change-management models that grew up beside these process disciplines — Kurt Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze, John Kotter's eight steps, Prosci's ADKAR — supplied the human side: how to get people to accept the new process once it had been designed.

The 2015 wheel draws on all of these without belonging wholly to any. Its first two stages, Discover and Define and Design, are the analytical front end of every re-engineering programme. Its third, Review, is the challenge function that the financial-services industry institutionalised after the 2008 crisis. Its fourth, Process Mapping, is the documentary heart of the BPM tradition. Its fifth, Prototype and Test, borrows from design thinking and agile. Its sixth, Business Acceptance, is the governance gate familiar from every PRINCE2 project board. Its seventh, Embedding, is Lewin's refreeze. And its coda, Continuous Improvement, returns to Deming. It is, in other words, an intelligent synthesis — a practitioner's distillation of thirty years of process discipline into a form that a regulated organisation can use.

1.2 The logic of the wheel

Every methodology encodes a theory, and the theory of the wheel can be reconstructed from its glosses with some precision.

First, there is a current state that can be understood. The first stage instructs the practitioner to engage, observe and understand the current state and requirements. The grammar presumes that the current state is a thing — singular, definite, available to observation — and that requirements are likewise given, waiting to be gathered.

Second, there is a right solution that can be designed. The second stage asks the practitioner to define operational and regulatory needs and design the right process solution. Not a good solution, not a workable one, not one of several: the right one. The definite article carries an epistemology. It says that the problem has an answer, and that the answer can be reached by design before it is tried.

Third, consequences can be anticipated. The third stage, Review, asks the practitioner to challenge assumptions and consider impact and knock-on effects. This is the most self-aware stage of the wheel, and it deserves credit for that. But the phrase knock-on effects presumes a particular causal picture: one thing knocks another, which knocks a third, in a chain that can be followed forward from the intervention.

Fourth, reality can be validated against a map. The fourth stage instructs the practitioner to map the process, walk through, validate what is really happening. Here the wheel admits — again to its credit — that the documented process and the real process may diverge. But it resolves the divergence by validation: the map is checked against reality and corrected until they agree. The map is a copy; reality is the original.

Fifth, testing converges. The fifth stage asks the practitioner to test in practice, gather feedback, revalidate and refine. The verbs are iterative, but the iteration has a direction: towards the designed solution, refined. One prototype is tested, adjusted, tested again.

Sixth, legitimacy is conferred from above. The sixth stage requires the change to be agreed through governance and owner sign-off. Agreement is located in a governance body and an owner — singular — whose signature converts a proposal into an authorised process.

Seventh, variance is deviation. The seventh stage asks the practitioner to embed, monitor and enable, and concludes with the most revealing phrase on the page: errors become exceptions. Once the process is embedded, whatever does not conform to it is reclassified — no longer an error in the design, but an exception to an otherwise working rule.

Eighth, improvement comes after. The coda places continuous improvement post embedding: test, assure, feedback, remediate, assess materiality, evolve techniques. Improvement is a phase, and it follows closure.

Laid out like this, the theory is coherent and familiar. It is the theory of the engineer, and it is a good theory — for things that are engineered.

1.3 What the wheel gets right

A disputation that cannot say what its opponent gets right has not understood its opponent. The wheel gets at least five things right, and the counter-design keeps all five.

It insists on looking before acting. Discover and Explore is not a formality. Countless change programmes fail because they begin with a solution and go looking for a problem to apply it to. The wheel's insistence that the practitioner first engage and observe is the beginning of humility.

It builds in challenge. Review — challenge assumptions — institutionalises dissent. In a sector whose great failures (the mis-selling scandals, the 2008 crisis, the LIBOR affair) were failures of challenge as much as failures of control, that matters.

It distinguishes the documented from the real. The gloss on Process Mapping — validate what is really happening — is the wheel's single most important sentence. It concedes that procedures describe one world and practice inhabits another. The safety scientist Erik Hollnagel calls this the gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done, and he regards it as the central fact of organisational life. The wheel sees the gap. Where it goes wrong, as we shall see, is in how it proposes to close it.

It tests in practice. Prototype and Test refuses to let a design go live untried. That is the empirical conscience of the methodology.

It does not stop at go-live. The coda of continuous improvement acknowledges that embedding is not the end. Many methodologies stop at the handover; this one at least gestures beyond it.

These are real virtues, and they explain why the wheel has lasted ten years. A practitioner who followed it faithfully would do better than one who followed nothing. The question this book asks is not whether the wheel is better than nothing. It is whether the wheel is adequate to the world in which governance, risk and compliance now operate — and if it is not, what would be.

1.4 The world the wheel was drawn for

The methodology is dated, precisely, 15 January 2015. It is worth recalling what the regulated world looked like that winter, and what it has looked like since.

In January 2015 the Financial Conduct Authority was not yet two years old. The Senior Managers and Certification Regime, which would relocate accountability from committees to named individuals, had been legislated for but not yet brought into force for banks. The General Data Protection Regulation had not been finalised. Operational resilience, as a supervisory discipline with impact tolerances and important business services, did not yet exist in its later form. The Consumer Duty, with its demand that firms evidence good outcomes rather than merely compliant processes, lay eight years in the future. Generative artificial intelligence was a research curiosity. Remote working was an exception. The pandemic was unimagined.

Every one of these developments has pressed in the same direction. The SM&CR moved accountability from the structure to the person and asked each senior manager to show that they had taken reasonable steps — a standard that cannot be met by pointing at a process map. Operational resilience asked firms to assume that disruption will happen and to show that they can remain within tolerance when it does — a shift from preventing failure to living with it. The Consumer Duty asked firms to evidence outcomes, which is to say, to look past the process to what it actually produces in the lives of customers. Each of these is, in its own vocabulary, a move from the ordered to the complex: from asking whether the process was followed to asking what actually happened; from treating the firm as a designed machine to treating it as a living system whose behaviour must be continually sensed.

The wheel was drawn for a world in which a methodology could reasonably promise to design the right process, have it signed off, and embed it. That world was never quite real. It is now visibly gone.

1.5 The leak, read closely

The Prologue noted that the wheel's segments, read clockwise, run 1–2–3–7–6–5–4. It is worth dwelling on this, because the leak is a better guide to the reality of change than the numbering is.

Consider what the clockwise reading implies. After Review (3), one comes immediately to Embedding (7). After Embedding, Business Acceptance (6). After acceptance, Prototype and Test (5). After testing, Process Mapping (4). And after mapping — back to Discover (1). Read this way, the wheel tells a different and arguably truer story: that one reviews, then finds that something has already embedded itself; that acceptance is negotiated after the fact, around what people are already doing; that testing happens on what has already been accepted; that mapping is the attempt to describe what testing has revealed; and that the map, once drawn, opens a new round of discovery.

This is not a fanciful reading. It is close to what anyone who has run a change programme in a regulated firm will recognise. The process that the governance committee signs off is very often the process that the operations team has already been running for six months, formalised. The map is very often drawn after the test, to document what the test found. Discovery never stops; it is going on in every corridor conversation. The numbered sequence is the official story; the clockwise sequence is closer to the shadow story. And the shadow story is not a corruption of the official one. It is how organisations actually change.

Ralph Stacey, whose work on complex responsive processes of relating underlies much of this book, makes exactly this distinction between the legitimate system — the official, formal, documented organisation — and the shadow system, the network of informal relationships, conversations and improvisations through which most of the real work gets done. Stacey's point is not that the shadow system is bad and should be eliminated, nor that it is good and should replace the legitimate system. It is that the two are in perpetual tension, and that novelty — genuine change — emerges in the shadow system before it is ever acknowledged by the legitimate one. A methodology that only sees the legitimate system will always be describing changes that have already happened somewhere else.

1.6 Why a cycle is still a line

The wheel's defenders could reply that the methodology is a cycle precisely in order to avoid linearity. It loops; it repeats; it returns. Does the circular form not already answer the complexity critique?

It does not, and the reason is worth stating carefully. A cycle is a line whose end has been joined to its beginning. It remains a sequence: each stage has a predecessor and a successor, and there is only one way round. It remains centred: everything turns about a fixed point, which in the 2015 diagram is literally occupied by the name of the methodology and its four virtues. It remains closed: there is no way in or out except at the designated entry point, and nothing on the rim connects to anything other than its two neighbours. And it remains repetitive in the bad sense: the cycle returns to the same place, as a wheel returns to the same point on its rim with every revolution.

Complexity science is not opposed to cycles. Feedback loops are its bread and butter. But the feedback loops of complex systems are not single and closed; they are many, nested, crossing, and they produce not return but novelty. Ilya Prigogine's dissipative structures — the convection cells in heated fluid, the oscillating colours of the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction — maintain themselves through cycles of energy and matter, but those cycles take the system further from equilibrium, not back to where it began, and at critical thresholds they bifurcate into wholly new forms. Edgar Morin's recursive loop is a cycle in which the products are also the producers of what produces them: individuals make society, which makes individuals. Such loops do not turn; they spiral, and the spiral does not return to its origin.

The wheel's circularity is therefore a geometrical, not a dynamical, answer to the problem of linearity. It bends the line; it does not open it.

1.7 The question of domain

None of this means the wheel should be thrown away. The most useful single idea in recent complexity thinking for practitioners is the idea of domain: that different kinds of situation require different kinds of response, and that the commonest failure is not to have the wrong method but to apply the right method in the wrong place.

Dave Snowden's Cynefin framework, which he developed at IBM around 1999 and published with Cynthia Kurtz in 2003, distinguishes ordered domains — the clear, where cause and effect are obvious and best practice applies, and the complicated, where cause and effect can be discovered by expert analysis and good practice applies — from unordered domains: the complex, where cause and effect can only be perceived in retrospect and the right response is to probe, sense and respond; and the chaotic, where there is no perceivable relationship between cause and effect and the right response is to act first to establish some stability. Between them lies confusion, or aporia — not knowing which domain one is in.

Read through Cynefin, the 2015 wheel is an excellent instrument for the complicated domain. Discover, design, review, map, test, accept, embed: this is exactly what expert analysis looks like when it is done well. Its errors begin only when it is applied to the complex domain — to situations in which the current state is not stable enough to be understood, in which there is no right solution to be designed in advance, in which knock-on effects cannot be anticipated, in which the map changes the territory, in which tests diverge rather than converge, in which legitimacy is negotiated rather than conferred, and in which errors are signals rather than exceptions.

The trouble is that most of what a GRC function now governs lies in the complex domain. Conduct risk — the risk that a firm's behaviour produces poor outcomes for customers — is emergent from countless interactions and cannot be designed out. Operational resilience is, by definition, about the unanticipated. Culture, which every regulator now asks firms to evidence, is the paradigm of a complex phenomenon. Cyber threat is adversarial and adaptive. Even credit risk, which once looked complicated (models, ratings, provisions), reveals its complexity in every downturn, when correlations that were stable for a decade suddenly change sign. The wheel is a good tool. It is being asked to do a job it was not built for.

2The Arborescent Image of Thought

The tree in the background of the wheel is not an ornament. It is a philosophy. This chapter reconstructs what Deleuze and Guattari mean by arborescence, shows how deeply it structures the governance of regulated organisations, and explains why they set against it the strange, low-growing, sideways figure of the rhizome.

2.1 Three kinds of book, three kinds of root

The introduction to A Thousand Plateaus, first published separately in 1976 under the title Rhizome, opens with a typology of books, and the typology is botanical.

The first kind is the root-book. It is organised like a taproot: one principal root from which everything descends. The classical book, the book as an image of the world, proceeds from a single principle and unfolds it — a binary logic in which one becomes two, two become four, and every branch can be traced back to the trunk. This is the logic of genealogy, of classification, of the tree of Porphyry that sorts beings into genera and species by successive division. Its unity is given in advance; the multiplicity it produces is derived from that unity and always subordinate to it.

The second kind is the fascicular root or radicle-system. Here the principal root has been aborted or destroyed, and a mass of secondary roots grows instead. This looks like a liberation from unity, and modernist literature — the fragment, the cut-up, the collage — seemed to practise it. But Deleuze and Guattari argue that the fascicular system only displaces the unity: the world has lost its pivot, but the book recovers a higher, hidden unity — a unity of totality, or of the author's subjectivity, or of the system that guarantees the fragments belong together. Postmodern management thinking that celebrates fragmentation while keeping a strategy office at the centre is fascicular in exactly this sense.

The third kind is the rhizome — the underground stem of couch grass, ginger, bamboo, iris; the burrow of rats; the swarm; the tuber. A rhizome has no centre and no principal root. It spreads laterally; any point can connect to any other; it can be broken at any point and will regrow from that point or another. It does not reproduce by descent from a parent but by variation, expansion, conquest, capture, offshoots. It is not a multiplicity derived from a unity. It is a multiplicity that has no unity at all, and that does not need one.

The value of this typology for governance is that it lets us see three different ways an organisation can understand itself. The arborescent organisation knows its root (the board, the founder, the mission statement) and derives everything else from it by delegation and decomposition. The fascicular organisation has decentralised, empowered, flattened — but has secretly kept a unity in the form of a culture programme, a single platform, a common framework. The rhizomatic organisation has no secret centre. It is held together by its connections, not by its derivation.

2.2 Arborescence in the regulated firm

It is not difficult to find the tree in a regulated firm. It is drawn on every organisation chart.

The governance tree runs from the board through its committees to executive management and down through divisions, departments and teams. Authority descends; information ascends. Each node has one parent. The chart is a genealogy: to understand any role, trace it back to the root.

The policy tree runs from a risk appetite statement through a policy framework to individual policies, standards, procedures and work instructions. Each document derives its authority from the one above. The procedure is legitimate because it implements a standard, which implements a policy, which implements an appetite, which the board approved.

The risk taxonomy is a tree in the strictest Porphyrian sense: risk divides into financial and non-financial; non-financial into operational, conduct, compliance, strategic; operational into the Basel event types; each event type into sub-types. Every risk has its place in the classification, and its place determines its owner, its controls, its reporting line.

The control tree maps controls to risks to processes to objectives, in a cascade that allows any control failure to be traced up to the objective it threatens.

The accountability tree, sharpened since the SM&CR, maps every prescribed responsibility to a named senior manager, whose statement of responsibilities fits into a management responsibilities map that covers the whole firm without gaps or overlaps.

The Three Lines Model — formerly the three lines of defence — is itself a tree turned sideways: management owns risk, the second line oversees it, internal audit assures it, the board sits above all three. Each line has its role; the roles are distinct; the hierarchy of assurance is clear.

The 2015 wheel fits into these trees perfectly. Its Discover stage feeds requirements up to the design authority; its Business Acceptance stage is the point at which the tree confers its authority on the change; its Embedding stage installs the new process in its place in the process tree; its post-embedding stage feeds assurance up to the second and third lines. The wheel is the tree in motion — the procedure by which a new branch is grafted on.

2.3 What the tree is good for, and what it cannot see

Deleuze and Guattari are not naive about trees. They do not say that arborescence is false or that it should be abolished. They say that it is an image of thought — a way of organising thinking that has become so dominant that it is mistaken for thinking itself — and that it systematically hides certain things.

The tree is good for tracing lineage. If one needs to know who authorised a decision, or which policy a procedure implements, or which risk a control addresses, the tree is indispensable. Regulators need this, and they are right to.

The tree is good for allocating responsibility. In a complex system responsibility tends to dissolve: everyone contributed, so nobody is to blame. The SM&CR was a deliberate re-imposition of arborescence on precisely this dissolution, after the 2008 crisis revealed that senior bankers could preside over catastrophe without being personally accountable for any of it. The tree, here, is a moral achievement.

The tree is good for ensuring coverage. A taxonomy that divides the whole field without remainder guarantees that every risk has been thought about by someone.

But the tree cannot see five things, and they are the five things that matter most in complex situations.

It cannot see lateral connection. In the tree, every node connects only to its parent and its children. The control in the payments team and the control in the complaints team are cousins at best, related only through a common ancestor three levels up. But in the real firm they may be intimately connected — the complaints team may be the first to notice when the payments control fails, the payments team may have built a workaround that the complaints team depends on — and that connection is invisible on any tree.

It cannot see heterogeneity. The tree classifies like with like. Risks sit in the risk taxonomy; controls in the control library; people in the organisation chart; systems in the application inventory. But what actually produces an outcome is always a heterogeneous mixture: this person, using this system, applying this rule, under this time pressure, in this mood, with this customer. The tree has no place for the mixture. It disaggregates it into its classified components and loses the thing that mattered.

It cannot see multiplicity. The tree derives the many from the one. It assumes that behind the plurality of procedures there is one policy, behind the plurality of risks one appetite, behind the plurality of roles one board. But much of what happens in a firm has no such unity. There is no single thing called the culture; there are many cultures, overlapping, contradictory, local. There is no single thing called the customer journey; there are as many journeys as customers.

It cannot see rupture. When a branch breaks, the tree loses it. When a control fails, the tree registers a failure. It has no way of seeing that the failure is also a beginning — that something else has started up at the point of breakage, that the workaround which replaced the failed control may be better than the control was.

It cannot see the map. The tree is a tracing: a reproduction of a structure that already exists. It describes. It does not intervene. It has no concept of a representation that changes what it represents.

These five blind spots correspond exactly to the six principles of the rhizome — connection, heterogeneity, multiplicity, asignifying rupture, cartography and decalcomania — which Chapter 5 will examine in detail. The rhizome is defined, point for point, as what the tree cannot see.

2.4 The image of thought

The phrase image of thought comes from Deleuze's earlier work, above all Difference and Repetition (1968), where Chapter 3 is devoted to it. It is worth understanding, because it explains why the arborescent methodology feels so natural that questioning it can seem perverse.

An image of thought is a set of unexamined presuppositions about what it means to think. Deleuze identifies eight postulates of the dominant Western image, and several of them map directly onto the wheel.

There is the postulate of good will: that thinking naturally seeks truth, and that error is an accident, a deviation from thought's proper course. This is the presupposition behind errors become exceptions. The process is assumed to aim naturally at correctness; error is what happens when it is deflected.

There is the postulate of recognition: that thinking is fundamentally a matter of recognising things — identifying an object as the same object across different faculties, saying this is a table. Deleuze argues that recognition is the least interesting thing thought does; real thinking begins with an encounter that cannot be recognised, something that forces us to think because it does not fit any category we have. The wheel's Discover stage is an act of recognition — understand the current state — and the risk is that it recognises only what it already expected to find.

There is the postulate of representation: that difference is always subordinated to identity — that we understand a thing by what it shares with others of its kind, and its differences are mere variations on a common form. This is the logic of the risk taxonomy, in which every risk is understood as an instance of a category.

There is the postulate of the model of knowledge: that the goal of thinking is a result, a piece of knowledge, rather than an ongoing process of learning. The wheel aims at an embedded process — a result — and treats learning as the instrumental means of getting there.

Against this image Deleuze sets what he calls a thought without image: a thinking that begins in an encounter, proceeds by problems rather than by solutions, values difference over identity, and treats learning as an end rather than a means. It is not difficult to see in this the outlines of complex-domain practice: probe rather than plan, sense what emerges rather than recognise what was expected, treat the situation as a problem to be lived rather than a puzzle to be solved.

Scholium 2

Deleuze's distinction between a problem and a solution is one of the most practically useful ideas in his philosophy. We are taught that problems exist in order to be solved, and that a problem disappears when its solution is found. Deleuze argues that a well-posed problem is never exhausted by its solutions; it persists, and generates new solutions as circumstances change. Consumer Duty is a good example. The problem — how to ensure that a firm's products and services produce good outcomes for customers — is not solved by any particular redesign; it persists through every redesign, and each solution reveals new aspects of the problem. A methodology that treats the problem as closed once a solution is embedded has misunderstood what kind of problem it is.

2.5 Arborescence as a politics

Deleuze and Guattari are explicit that the tree is not only a logic but a politics. Arborescent systems, they write, are hierarchical systems with centres of significance and subjectification, central automata like organised memories. In such systems an element receives information only from a higher unit and receives subjective affection only along pre-established paths.

That last phrase — along pre-established paths — describes with uncomfortable precision how information moves in a governed organisation. The risk event in the branch is reported to the branch manager, who reports to the regional head, who reports to the divisional risk committee, which reports to the executive risk committee, which reports to the board risk committee. At each step the information is summarised, filtered, aggregated. By the time it reaches the root it has become a red-amber-green status on a dashboard, and the branch staff who first noticed it have no idea what became of it. The path was pre-established; the information travelled along it; and everything that did not fit the path — the context, the hunch, the connection to something in another branch — was lost.

The financial crisis inquiries of 2009 to 2013 are full of examples. Concerns raised at the front line travelled up the tree and were filtered out before they reached anyone who could act. Lateral warnings — from one desk to another, from risk to trading, from one firm to its counterparty — had no path to travel on. The trees were perfectly drawn and the information did not flow.

Deleuze and Guattari's alternative is not to abolish paths but to multiply them: to build systems in which communication runs from any neighbour to any other, in which the stems and channels do not pre-exist, and in which the result is synchronised without a central agency. They draw here on the work of Pierre Rosenstiehl and Jean Petitot on acentred automata — networks of finite automata in which global coordination emerges from local interactions without a general. A firing squad can fire together without an officer, they note, if each soldier responds to his neighbours. Complexity science has since produced many such examples: flocks, swarms, slime moulds, markets, the internet. Each achieves coordination without arborescence.

2.6 Not tree or rhizome

It would be easy to read all this as a simple inversion: tree bad, rhizome good. Deleuze and Guattari explicitly refuse that reading, and so does this book.

They say that there are knots of arborescence in rhizomes and rhizomatic offshoots in roots. They say that a rhizome may contain points of structuration, territorialisation, hierarchisation, and that it is always at risk of reconstituting a tree. They say that the important question is not to choose between models but to see the processes by which one becomes the other — the tree that sprouts a rhizome, the rhizome that hardens into a tree. They even warn that the rhizome is not a programme for liberation: the same lateral connections that allow creativity also allow the spread of micro-fascisms, and a smooth space is no guarantee of anything good.

For governance this nuance is decisive. A regulated firm cannot become a pure rhizome and would be wrong to try. Some things must be arborescent: the line of personal accountability that runs from the board to a named senior manager; the chain of authorisation that prevents a single employee from moving customer money without a second signature; the classification of risks that ensures nothing falls through the cracks. These are the molar lines of Part III, held rigid because the law, the public interest and ordinary prudence require it.

The error of the wheel is not that it contains trees. It is that it contains only trees — that it takes the arborescent form, which is appropriate for some things, and applies it to everything, including the things that are not and cannot be arborescent. The counter-design's answer is not to uproot the tree but to see it for what it is: one formation among others, a hardening within a wider tangle, necessary in some places and stifling in others. The question every practitioner must ask is where the tree belongs and where it does not.

This is why the third diagram ends with the injunction to hold the tree where it is owed. The rhizome does not abolish the obligations that the tree encodes. It situates them.

2.7 The tree as a defence against anxiety

One more thing must be said about arborescence before we leave it, and it comes not from Deleuze but from the psychoanalytic tradition that Stacey and his colleagues drew upon. Why is the tree so attractive? Why does every organisation reach for it, even when it plainly does not fit?

Isabel Menzies Lyth's classic study of nursing in a London teaching hospital, published in 1960, found that much of the hospital's elaborate procedure — the task lists, the rotation of nurses between wards, the depersonalisation of patients as the liver in bed ten — served less to deliver care than to protect nurses from the anxiety of caring for the sick and dying. The procedures were a social defence system. They worked, in the sense that they reduced anxiety; but they also reduced the quality of care and the satisfaction of the nurses, and they were extraordinarily resistant to change, because changing them would release the anxiety they contained.

The arborescent methodology functions in part as a social defence of the same kind. Change in a regulated firm is frightening. It exposes people to regulatory sanction, to personal liability, to the possibility of harming customers, to the loss of their jobs. A methodology that promises to make change orderly — to discover, design, review, map, test, accept, embed — contains that anxiety. It says: if you follow the stages, you will be safe. The promise is not quite true, but it is comforting, and the comfort is real. Ralph Stacey makes the same point about strategic plans: much of their value, he suggests, lies not in their predictive accuracy, which is generally poor, but in their capacity to reduce anxiety enough for people to act.

This matters for the counter-design, because a rhizomatic practice that simply removed the tree would also remove the defence, and the anxiety would have to go somewhere. Chapter 9 returns to this question under the heading of containment: how a governance practice that admits it does not control outcomes can nonetheless hold the anxiety of the people inside it well enough for them to keep thinking. The short answer is that the rhizome must offer its own forms of holding — the refrain, the consent record, the line register — which contain anxiety not by pretending to certainty but by giving people something stable to stand on while the ground moves.

3Order Mistaken for the World

The wheel's deepest assumption is not stated on the page, because it is the condition of everything that is. It is that the world into which the change is introduced is ordered — that it can be known, designed, predicted and controlled. This chapter sets that assumption against what complexity science has learned about systems that are not ordered, and then turns to the motto that crowns the reissue: Principles endure.

3.1 What makes a system complex

Paul Cilliers, the South African philosopher whose Complexity and Postmodernism (1998) did more than any other book to bring complexity science and post-structuralist philosophy into conversation, offered a list of characteristics of complex systems that has become something of a standard. Paraphrased and condensed, it runs as follows.

  • Complex systems consist of a large number of elements, and the number matters: below a certain threshold, the system can be described element by element.
  • The elements interact dynamically, and the interactions are rich — any element influences and is influenced by many others.
  • The interactions are non-linear: small causes can have large effects, and large causes small ones.
  • The interactions are typically short-range: each element responds mainly to its neighbours, not to the whole.
  • There are loops in the interactions — feedback, positive and negative, direct and indirect.
  • Complex systems are open: they exchange energy and information with their environment, and it is often hard to say where the system ends.
  • They operate far from equilibrium: a constant flow is needed to maintain their organisation.
  • They have a history: their past is co-responsible for their present behaviour.
  • Each element is ignorant of the behaviour of the system as a whole: it responds only to the information available to it locally.

Apply this list to a regulated firm and every item fits. Thousands of employees, customers, systems, rules. Rich interactions across departments, channels and counterparties. Non-linear effects — a single mis-sold product line can produce a remediation programme costing billions. Short-range interactions — most people respond to their immediate team, their manager, their queue, not to the strategy. Feedback loops everywhere — incentive schemes that reshape behaviour that reshapes the incentive scheme. Openness to markets, regulators, the press, social media. Far-from-equilibrium operation — stop the flow of transactions and the firm ceases to exist. A history that shapes every decision. And, above all, local ignorance: nobody in the firm, not the chief executive, not the chief risk officer, not the board, knows the behaviour of the system as a whole.

That last point is the decisive one, and Cilliers draws out its implication with great clarity. If no element of a complex system has access to the whole, then no element can represent the whole. Any model of a complex system — any process map, any risk register, any methodology — is necessarily a simplification, and the simplification is not innocent: it leaves out things that may turn out to matter, and there is no way of knowing in advance which. Cilliers calls this the problem of incompressibility. A complex system cannot be described more simply than by the system itself, because any simpler description discards interactions whose consequences cannot be known until they occur.

This is why the wheel's first stage, understand the current state, is not merely difficult in the complex domain but impossible in principle. There is no current state available to understanding. There are only partial views, each from a particular location in the system, each leaving things out.

3.2 Cynefin revisited

Chapter 1 introduced Cynefin as a framework of domains. It is worth returning to it, because the framework's dynamics — the ways in which situations move between domains — are as important as its static categories, and the wheel has no account of them.

In Snowden's later formulations, the boundary between the clear and the chaotic domains is drawn as a cliff. Situations that are treated as clear — governed by best practice, routinised, unexamined — become complacent; complacency hides the accumulation of small deviations; and at some point the accumulated deviation tips the situation over the cliff into chaos. The pattern is familiar from every major operational failure. The process ran for years; everyone knew it worked; nobody looked closely; and then one day it did not work, catastrophically, and the organisation discovered that the conditions on which it depended had been eroding for a long time.

The 2015 wheel is designed to deliver processes to the clear domain. Its terminal stage, Embedding — embed, monitor and enable; errors become exceptions — is precisely the move that converts a designed process into a routine, best-practice operation. The danger is that it delivers processes to the edge of the cliff. A process that has been embedded so thoroughly that its errors are treated as exceptions, rather than as signals, is a process that has stopped looking at itself. It has become complacent in the technical sense.

Snowden's prescription for the complex domain — probe, sense, respond — is the reverse of the wheel's sequence. The wheel says: understand, then design, then test. The complex domain says: act first, in small safe-to-fail ways, then sense what happens, then respond by amplifying what works and dampening what does not. Understanding comes after action, not before; and it is understanding of patterns, not of a state. The wheel's Prototype and Test stage gestures at this, but places it fifth in the sequence, after understanding, design, review and mapping have already committed the practitioner to a particular solution. In the complex domain, probing is not the fifth stage. It is the first, and the last, and every stage in between.

Scholium 3

Snowden draws a distinction between fail-safe and safe-to-fail design that maps directly onto the difference between the wheel and the rhizome. Fail-safe design tries to ensure that nothing goes wrong, by anticipating every failure mode and engineering it out. It is appropriate where failure modes can be known. Safe-to-fail design accepts that things will go wrong, and arranges matters so that when they do, the damage is contained and the learning is captured. It is appropriate where failure modes cannot be known in advance. A regulated firm needs both: fail-safe controls on the molar lines (payment authorisation, capital calculation) and safe-to-fail probes on the molecular and flight lines (new customer journeys, new uses of data, new forms of vulnerability support).

3.3 The Stacey matrix and its withdrawal

Before Cynefin, the most widely used device for sorting situations by complexity was the matrix associated with Ralph Stacey. It plots situations on two axes: agreement among the people involved (from close to far) and certainty about cause and effect (from close to far). Near the origin — high agreement, high certainty — lies the zone of technical rationality, where planning and monitoring work. Far from the origin — low agreement, low certainty — lies anarchy. In between lies a broad band that came to be called the zone of complexity, where neither planning nor anarchy is adequate and where creativity and innovation occur.

The matrix was widely adopted in management education, and it is still reproduced in countless slide decks. What is less widely known is that Stacey himself withdrew it. By the early 2000s he had come to think that the matrix was misleading, because it implied that a manager could stand outside a situation, locate it on the grid, and then select the appropriate tool — that complexity was a region of the map to be entered or avoided at will. His mature view, developed with Douglas Griffin and Patricia Shaw in the complex responsive processes tradition, was that all human organising is complex, all the time; that there is no outside position from which to classify it; and that what looks like the zone of technical rationality is simply a region where people have, for the moment, agreed to act as if things were certain.

This withdrawal matters for the argument of this book in two ways. First, it warns against using Cynefin or any similar framework as a sorting machine that tells you which method to apply. The practitioner is always already inside the situation, and her classification of it is itself an intervention in it. Second, it suggests that the ordered domain is not a separate territory but a formation within the complex — a temporary stabilisation, a region where things have been made to hold still by continuous effort. This is very close to Deleuze and Guattari's claim that trees are knots within rhizomes. The ordered is not the opposite of the complex; it is one of the things the complex can do.

An earlier treatise in this series, on the Stacey matrix and the garden at Ryōan-ji, argued that there is no view from nowhere: that every location on the matrix is a location for someone, seen from somewhere, and that the matrix's apparent objectivity conceals the situated knowledges of those who draw it. The present book takes that conclusion as given. The wheel is drawn from somewhere — from the position of the change consultant who will design the process, have it signed off, and hand it over. A different drawer — the operations clerk who must live with the process, the customer who encounters it, the regulator who must supervise it — would draw a different figure.

3.4 Far from equilibrium

Ilya Prigogine received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1977 for his work on the thermodynamics of systems far from equilibrium. His central finding, popularised in Order out of Chaos (1984, with Isabelle Stengers), was that systems through which energy and matter flow can spontaneously generate order — not despite their distance from equilibrium, but because of it. These dissipative structures maintain themselves by continuously dissipating energy; cut off the flow and they collapse back into equilibrium, which is to say, into death.

Two features of dissipative structures are especially relevant to governance.

The first is bifurcation. As a dissipative structure is pushed further from equilibrium, it reaches critical points at which its existing organisation becomes unstable and it must choose between two or more new organisations. Which it chooses depends on fluctuations at the moment of bifurcation — tiny, local, unpredictable variations that are amplified into the system's new macro-structure. Before the bifurcation, these fluctuations are noise. At the bifurcation, they decide the future.

For a governance practice, this means that the small deviations which the wheel reclassifies as exceptions — the near miss, the odd complaint, the workaround — are not necessarily noise. At the right moment, one of them may be the fluctuation that determines which way the organisation goes. A practice that suppresses them is suppressing the very variations from which its future will be selected.

The second feature is history. Because bifurcations are decided by fluctuations, the path a dissipative structure takes is irreversible and unique. Two identical systems subjected to the same conditions may end up in different states, because they took different branches at a bifurcation. Prigogine called this the end of certainty: the realisation that even in physics, the future is not contained in the present. For organisations, it means that no methodology can promise the same results in different firms, or even in the same firm at different times. Best practice is a concept that assumes reversibility. In a world of bifurcations, there is only practice that worked here, then.

3.5 The adjacent possible

Stuart Kauffman, the theoretical biologist whose work on self-organisation and the origins of life underpins much of complexity science, introduced a concept that is especially useful for thinking about change: the adjacent possible. At any moment, a system can move into a set of states that are one step away from its present state — combinations of what already exists that have not yet been made. As the system moves into one of these states, the adjacent possible expands: new combinations become available that were not available before. The space of possibilities is not fixed in advance; it grows as it is explored.

The adjacent possible has a striking implication for design. It means that the right process solution — the target of the wheel's second stage — does not exist in advance of the process of finding it. It is not out there waiting to be discovered by sufficiently careful analysis. It comes into existence as the organisation moves through its adjacent possible, and each move changes what the next moves can be. The designer who sits down to design the right solution before acting is like a chess player who tries to plan the whole game from the opening position: the space is too large, and it changes with every move.

Kauffman also emphasised that the adjacent possible is reached by tinkering with what already exists, not by designing from first principles. Evolution does not invent the eye from scratch; it modifies a light-sensitive patch, then a cup, then a pinhole, each step building on the last. Exaptation — the co-option of a structure evolved for one function to serve another — is one of evolution's most powerful moves. Snowden has made exaptation central to his account of innovation in organisations: new capabilities are found, more often than designed, in the repurposing of things that already exist. The workaround that the wheel would reclassify as an exception is very often an exaptation in progress.

3.6 Principles endure: Deleuze on difference and repetition

We come now to the motto of the reissue: Principles endure. Practice evolves. It is a modest and reasonable claim, and it is meant reassuringly. It says that the methodology's foundations are sound, and that what has changed over ten years is only their application. The counter-design replaces it with its opposite: Principles don't endure — they differ. Practice is how they repeat. The replacement is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the centre of Deleuze's philosophy, and it changes what a principle is.

The philosophical tradition that Deleuze opposes — he traces it from Plato through Aristotle to Hegel — treats difference as secondary to identity. There are identities first (forms, essences, concepts, principles), and differences are the variations between instances of an identity, or the negations that distinguish one identity from another. A principle, in this tradition, is an identity: it stays the same, and its applications differ from one another only in the circumstances to which it is applied. Principles endure, practice evolves is a perfect statement of this view.

Deleuze inverts it. Difference, he argues, is primary; identity is a secondary effect, produced when differences are organised in a certain way. There are no fixed forms behind the variety of things; there is a field of differences, and what we call identities are relatively stable patterns within it. And repetition — the recurrence of the same — is not the recurrence of an identity but the recurrence of a difference. What returns, in Deleuze's reading of Nietzsche's eternal return, is not the same but the different: each repetition is a new production, and it produces something new.

Consider what this means for a principle such as treat customers fairly. On the identity view, the principle is the same in 2015 and 2025; what changes is only how it is applied. On the difference view, the principle has no existence apart from its repetitions. It exists in each decision a frontline agent makes about a vulnerable customer, each product design choice, each complaint resolution, each supervisory challenge. And each of these repetitions is different — not a variation on a fixed form, but a fresh production of the principle under new conditions, which changes what the principle is. The treat customers fairly of 2025, after the Consumer Duty, after the pandemic's revelation of mass financial vulnerability, after the cost-of-living crisis, is not the same principle applied to new circumstances. It is a different principle, produced by the repetitions that have occurred in the meantime, and it would be a serious error to treat it as identical to its 2015 ancestor.

The practical consequence is that a governance practice must stop trying to preserve its principles and start attending to how they are repeated. The question is not are we still applying our principles? but what are our principles becoming, through the ways we are repeating them? A principle that is repeated mechanically — applied by rote, checked off a list — will become something thin and defensive, a box to be ticked. A principle that is repeated thoughtfully, in genuine encounter with each new situation, will become something richer. The principle is not the foundation of the practice. The practice is the life of the principle.

3.7 Two kinds of repetition

Deleuze distinguishes two kinds of repetition, and the distinction will be useful throughout Part III.

Bare repetition is the repetition of the same: the machine stamping out identical parts, the procedure followed step by step, the control tested by the same sample every quarter. It is the repetition of identity, and its ideal is zero variance.

Clothed repetition — Deleuze's term is répétition vêtue — is the repetition that carries difference within it: the musical theme that returns transformed, the ritual that is performed anew each time, the tradition that stays alive by changing. Its ideal is not zero variance but the right variance — variation that keeps the repetition alive.

A healthy governance practice needs both. On the molar lines — capital calculations, payment authorisations, regulatory reporting — bare repetition is exactly what is wanted. Variance there is a defect. On the molecular lines — frontline judgement, customer conversations, the interpretation of policy in particular cases — clothed repetition is essential. A complaints handler who resolves every complaint identically is not being consistent; she is failing to listen. The art of governance in a complex domain is knowing which kind of repetition each line requires, and the wheel, which knows only bare repetition, cannot help with that.

3.8 The domain restored

We can now state precisely what the disputation claims. The 2015 wheel is an instrument of the ordered domain, built on an image of thought that treats the organisation as a tree, knowledge as recognition, principles as identities and error as deviation. In the ordered domain — where cause and effect are knowable, where the current state is stable enough to be understood, where a right solution exists and can be designed — it is a fine instrument, and the counter-design keeps it for that domain.

But the world it governs is mostly not ordered. It is complex: incompressible, non-linear, far from equilibrium, historical, locally ignorant. In that world the wheel's assumptions fail, and they fail in the specific way that Snowden's cliff describes: the wheel delivers processes into a state of embedded complacency from which the slide into chaos is invisible until it happens.

The task of the next chapter is to take each stage of the wheel in turn and show what it becomes when it is relocated from the ordered domain to the complex — when the tree is opened into a rhizome.

4.1 A note on the word plateau

Deleuze and Guattari borrowed the word plateau from Gregory Bateson, who used it in his ethnography of Bali to describe a peculiar feature of Balinese social and erotic life: a tendency for intensities to be sustained at a continuous level rather than built up towards a climax and released. Western narrative, Bateson thought, is organised around climax — tension rises, peaks, resolves. Balinese culture, as he observed it, organised intensity differently: as a steady state, a plateau, which could be entered and left but which did not culminate.

Deleuze and Guattari took the word to name a region of continuous intensity that is not oriented towards a culmination or an external end. Each of the fifteen chapters of A Thousand Plateaus is a plateau in this sense: it can be read on its own, it connects with the others, and it does not build towards a finale. The book as a whole has no climax.

The word fits the transformation proposed here exactly. A stage is a step towards something — a position in a sequence whose meaning lies in what it leads to. Discover leads to Define, Define leads to Review, and the whole sequence culminates in Embedding, the climax of the change. A plateau is a region of activity that has its own value, that can be entered whenever it is needed, that is sustained rather than completed, and that connects laterally to every other. Discovery is not finished when design begins; it continues, at its own intensity, throughout. Embedding is not the culmination; it is one plateau among others, and the work may well pass through it many times. Nothing in the counter-design is ever complete. Everything is sustained.

4.2 Discover / Exploration → Enter in the middle

What the stage says. Engage, observe and understand the current state and requirements.

What it takes for granted. That there is a current state — a single, definite condition of the organisation that holds still long enough to be observed and understood. That requirements exist prior to the change and can be gathered. And that the practitioner stands, at least initially, outside the thing she observes: she engages with it, but as a visitor who will later act upon it.

What complexity knows. Cilliers's incompressibility argument (Chapter 3) means that no description of the current state can be complete; every description is a partial view from somewhere, leaving out interactions whose significance cannot be known in advance. Prigogine's far-from-equilibrium systems do not have a state in the static sense; they have a regime of flows, and what looks like a state is a pattern sustained by continuous activity. Stacey's work adds that the practitioner is never outside: the moment she begins to observe, she is a participant in the conversations she is observing, and her questions change what people say and do. There is no baseline. The very act of discovery is already an intervention.

The idea of requirements carries a parallel assumption. Requirements are treated as pre-existing needs that the change must satisfy. But in complex situations, what people need is partly discovered and partly created by the change itself. Before the new process exists, no one can say with confidence what they will need from it; their needs will form in their encounter with it. Requirements are not gathered. They emerge.

What the plateau becomes. Enter in the middle. Deleuze and Guattari insist that a rhizome has no beginning or end; it is always in the middle, between things. The practitioner does not begin; she joins. She enters a set of flows that were already moving before she arrived — conversations, workarounds, frustrations, adaptations — and her first task is not to fix a baseline but to sense what those flows are doing and where they are tending. François Jullien's account of shì, the Chinese concept of the propensity of things, is apt here: the skilled strategist does not impose a plan on a static situation but reads the situation's own tendencies and works with them.

Concretely, entering in the middle means at least three practical shifts. First, entry is triggered by events, not by the methodology: a complaint pattern, a regulatory letter, a near miss, a new product idea, a staff survey result. Any of these can be the door, and the door chooses which plateau is entered first — not always discovery. Second, discovery is distributed and continuous: rather than a team of analysts conducting a discovery phase, the organisation maintains a standing capacity to sense, through narrative capture, frontline listening, complaint analytics and the like, so that discovery is always already happening. Third, the practitioner records her own position: from where is she seeing? Whose account is she hearing? What would someone else see from elsewhere?

Keeps looking before acting. Drops the single knowable current state. Adds entry at any node, triggered by events, with the practitioner acknowledged as a participant.

THERE IS NO BASELINE. THERE IS ONLY WHERE YOU CAME IN.

4.3 Define and Design → Compose the assemblage

What the stage says. Define operational and regulatory needs and design the right process solution.

What it takes for granted. That needs can be defined before design begins. That there is a right solution — one, correct, findable by design. And, implicitly, that the solution is a process: a sequence of activities, specified in advance, that will produce the desired outcome when followed.

What complexity knows. Kauffman's adjacent possible means that the space of solutions is not fixed in advance; it grows as it is explored, and each move opens possibilities that did not exist before. The right solution does not exist waiting to be found. More fundamentally, the outcome a regulated firm cares about — a customer treated fairly, a payment made correctly, a vulnerable person supported, a breach prevented — is never produced by a process alone. It is produced by a heterogeneous combination: a person with certain skills, in a certain mood, using a certain system, applying a certain rule, under certain time pressure, in a certain relationship with a certain customer. The process is one component. Designing only the process is like designing only the score and expecting it to produce the performance.

What the plateau becomes. Compose the assemblage. Chapter 6 develops assemblage theory at length; here it is enough to say that an assemblage is a whole composed of heterogeneous parts — human and non-human, material and expressive — whose properties emerge from the relations between them. To compose an assemblage is to work with all the parts together: not only the process steps but the people who will perform them, the systems they will use, the data they will see, the rules they will apply, the incentives they will feel, the documents that will record what they do, the customers they will meet, and the moods — anxiety, confidence, boredom, pride — that will colour all of it.

Spinoza's question, which Deleuze made central to his reading of him, is what can a body do? — not what is it, but what is it capable of, in combination with what other bodies. The composition plateau asks the same question of the assemblage: what can this combination of people, rules and tools actually do? What outcomes can it produce? What can it not do, however well it is documented? The answer is found not by specifying the right solution but by trying out compositions and seeing what they are capable of — which is why Compose connects so densely, in the rhizome diagram, to Experiment in parallel.

The plural matters. There is no single right composition. There are several workable ones, each with different strengths, and the choice among them depends on things that cannot be known in advance. The plateau therefore produces a small portfolio of candidate compositions rather than one design.

The regulatory needs, however, are another matter. Some requirements are fixed: the law says what it says, and a composition that breaches it is not workable however capable it is. These are the molar lines, and the composition plateau treats them as constraints — the hard edges of the space within which compositions are explored. This is how the counter-design keeps the original's concern with regulatory need: not as one requirement among others to be traded off, but as the boundary of the search.

Keeps regulatory needs as fixed constraints. Drops the right solution, fixed in advance. Adds people, data, rules, systems and moods composed together, and a portfolio rather than a single design.

4.4 Review → Read the lines

What the stage says. Challenge assumptions. Consider impact and knock-on effects.

What it takes for granted. This is the most self-critical stage of the wheel, and the counter-design treats it with respect. But knock-on effects encodes a causal picture: interventions knock against things, which knock against other things, in a chain that can be followed forward. And challenge assumptions treats assumptions as discrete propositions that can be identified, tested and revised — as if the problem were a few false beliefs.

What complexity knows. In a non-linear system, effects do not travel down chains. They propagate through networks, amplifying in some places and damping in others, looping back to modify their own causes. The consequences of an intervention cannot be anticipated by following the chain forward, because there is no chain; there is a web, and the web is changed by the intervention. Diane Vaughan's study of the Challenger launch decision shows something further: the most dangerous assumptions are not explicit propositions that can be challenged but tacit patterns of practice — what she called the normalisation of deviance, the gradual acceptance of anomalies as normal, which no one decides and which therefore no one can challenge.

What the plateau becomes. Read the lines. Deleuze and Guattari's analysis of segmentarity (developed in Chapter 10) holds that every individual and every group is composed of three kinds of line: rigid lines of hard segmentation (molar), supple lines of molecular flux, and lines of flight that escape both. The review plateau becomes a practice of reading these lines. Which elements of the situation are rigid — fixed by law, by contract, by accountability, by physical constraint? Which are supple — practices, habits, interpretations that flex with circumstance? And which are already escaping — anomalies, workarounds, new behaviours that do not fit any existing category?

This is not merely a reclassification of risks. It changes what review is for. The wheel's review asks what could go wrong with our design? Reading the lines asks what kind of thing are we dealing with, and where? The answer determines the response: rigid lines are held, supple lines are attended to, lines of flight are followed. And it brings the normalisation of deviance into view. Deviance becomes normal when a line of flight — an anomaly — is quietly reclassified as part of the supple line of ordinary practice without anyone deciding that it should be. Reading the lines is the practice of noticing such reclassifications.

The challenge function survives. Indeed it is strengthened: independent challenge is precisely what is needed to read lines that the people inside the situation have stopped seeing. But challenge is no longer directed at a list of assumptions. It is directed at the segmentation itself — at the question of where the lines have been drawn and whether they are in the right place.

Keeps structured, independent challenge. Drops linear knock-on chains. Adds the sorting of the situation into rigid, supple and escaping lines.

4.5 Process Mapping → Map, don't trace

What the stage says. Map the process. Walk through. Validate what is really happening.

What it takes for granted. That a map is a representation of a reality that exists independently of it; that the map can be checked against that reality and corrected; and that once the map and the reality agree, the map can be used to manage the reality. The map is a copy. The reality is the original.

What complexity knows. This stage is where the wheel comes closest to the rhizome and where the difference between them is sharpest. Validate what is really happening concedes Hollnagel's gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done. But Hollnagel's further point is that work-as-done is not a fixed reality to be captured; it is a continuous adjustment to variable conditions, and it is precisely this adjustment that makes things go right most of the time. A map that captures one instance of work-as-done, validates it, and then fixes it as the new procedure has converted an adaptive practice back into a rigid one, and lost the adaptiveness that made it work. James C. Scott, in Seeing Like a State (1998), tells the same story at the scale of nations: the state's maps make the territory legible for administration, but in doing so they destroy the local, practical knowledge — mētis — that made the territory work.

What the plateau becomes. Map, don't trace. This is the one place in the counter-design where Deleuze and Guattari are quoted directly, because their phrasing cannot be bettered: make a map, not a tracing. A tracing, they explain, reproduces what is already there; it works by decalcomania, copying an existing structure. A map is something else: it is open, connectable in all its dimensions, detachable, reversible, susceptible to constant modification. It can be torn, adapted, reworked by an individual or a group. It is not a picture of the territory; it is an instrument for moving in it — and moving in it changes it.

For a GRC practice, the shift from tracing to mapping has concrete consequences. Maps are drawn with the people who do the work, not of them; they show the variations as well as the norm; they mark where the work flexes and why; they are dated and revisable; they record their own partiality (who drew this, from where); and they are used to provoke conversation about the work rather than to fix it. A process map in this sense is closer to what Snowden calls a fitness landscape or what Patricia Shaw describes as a conversation made visible than to a swim-lane diagram. The question it answers is not is this the process? but where could we go from here, and what would it take?

Chapter 7 returns to the six principles of the rhizome and shows that the last two — cartography and decalcomania — are specifically about this distinction.

Keeps walking the floor, and attention to work-as-done. Drops checking the map against an original. Adds maps as open, revisable, participatory interventions.

A TRACING TELLS YOU WHERE YOU ARE. A MAP TELLS YOU WHERE YOU COULD GO.

4.6 Prototype and Test → Experiment in parallel

What the stage says. Test in practice. Gather feedback. Revalidate and refine.

What it takes for granted. That the thing being tested is the designed solution; that testing is a means of refining it towards correctness; that feedback is information about the gap between the design and the reality; and that the process converges — each cycle of test and refine brings the solution closer to what it should be.

What complexity knows. In the complex domain, the purpose of an experiment is not to validate a design but to learn something about the system that could not have been learned any other way. A single pilot, tested in series, has two weaknesses. It commits early to one composition, so that everything learned is learned about that one; and it creates pressure for the pilot to succeed, because so much has been invested in it, which biases what feedback is heard. Snowden's safe-to-fail probes work differently. Several are run at once, deliberately different from one another, some of them designed to fail; each is small enough that failure is tolerable; and the point is to see which of them the system amplifies — which ones take — so that resources can be shifted towards those and away from the others.

What the plateau becomes. Experiment in parallel. A portfolio of probes, run simultaneously, each with a clear statement of what would count as a sign of success and what would count as a sign of failure, each small enough to be dropped. The plateau's characteristic actions are not test and refine but amplify and dampen: when a probe produces a pattern worth having, put more energy into it; when it produces a pattern worth avoiding, withdraw energy from it. The designed solution is not refined; the portfolio is evolved.

This changes what feedback is. In the wheel, feedback measures the gap between design and reality. In the plateau, feedback is the reality itself, speaking — the system telling you, through what it amplifies and what it ignores, what it is capable of. Deleuze's distinction between the problem and the solution (Scholium 2) applies: the experiment is not a test of a proposed solution but an exploration of the problem.

The regulatory constraint applies here with special force. Safe-to-fail is not license to harm customers. A probe that could breach a molar line — that could lead to unfair treatment, a data breach, a financial loss to a customer — is not safe-to-fail, however small. Part III develops the protocols that make parallel experimentation compatible with a regulated environment: probes are bounded by the molar lines, recorded in a probe log, reviewed for customer impact before and after, and stopped immediately if they begin to cross a line that must be held.

Keeps testing in practice, not on paper. Drops convergence on the pre-set design. Adds a portfolio of safe-to-fail probes, and the disciplines of amplifying and dampening.

4.7 Business Acceptance → Enunciate together

What the stage says. Agree through governance and owner sign-off.

What it takes for granted. That legitimacy is conferred, at a moment, by an authority — the governance forum and the process owner — and that once conferred it persists. That agreement is a state reached, rather than an activity sustained. And that the owner is singular: one person, at the end of one chain of delegation, whose signature makes the change official.

What complexity knows. The complex responsive processes tradition holds that organisations are not things but patterns of conversation, and that what counts as agreed is continuously negotiated in the ordinary interactions of people at work. A signature on a document does not make people do what the document says; what they actually do is shaped by the ongoing conversation about what the document means, whether it makes sense, and how it fits with everything else they are trying to do. Agreement is not a state. It is a practice. And it decays: what was agreed in March may be quietly abandoned by June if the conversation that sustained it falls silent.

Deleuze and Guattari have a concept for this. They speak of the collective assemblage of enunciation: the idea that utterances — statements, orders, policies — are never the product of an individual speaker but of a collective arrangement that makes certain statements possible and gives them their force. An order works not because the person who gives it has authority in some abstract sense, but because there is an assemblage — of institutions, practices, expectations, bodies — in which that statement, uttered by that person, has the effect of an order. Change the assemblage, and the same words lose their force.

What the plateau becomes. Enunciate together. Acceptance becomes a collective and renewable utterance. The plateau's work is to bring together the people whose consent actually determines whether the change takes hold — not only the governance forum and the owner, but the people who will do the work, the people who will be affected by it, and, where it matters, the customers — and to produce with them a statement of what is being agreed, on what conditions, for how long, and with what dissent.

Two points are critical. First, named accountability survives intact. The SM&CR requires a named senior manager to be responsible, and the counter-design does not dilute that; Chapter 9 argues that personal accountability is one of the molar knots that the rhizome must hold rigid. What changes is not who is accountable but how the accountable person discharges her responsibility: not by signing at a gate but by sustaining a consent that she can evidence. Second, dissent is recorded and kept. The wheel's sign-off closes the discussion; dissent that was voiced before it disappears from the record. The enunciation plateau keeps it, because dissent is very often the earliest form of a line of flight — the first sign that something the majority has not yet seen is happening.

Keeps named accountability under SM&CR. Drops sign-off as the end of discussion. Adds consent records, renewed as things move, with dissent kept on file.

4.8 Embedding → Territorialise — lightly

What the stage says. Embed, monitor and enable. Errors become exceptions.

What it takes for granted. That a change, once designed and accepted, should be installed permanently and then protected. That monitoring is the detection of deviation from the installed process. And — in the phrase that carries the whole weight of the wheel's epistemology — that errors, once the process is embedded, become exceptions: departures from an otherwise working rule, to be dealt with case by case, rather than signals that the rule itself might need to change.

What complexity knows. Chapter 3 showed how Snowden's cliff describes the trajectory of embedded processes: complacency, accumulation of unseen deviation, and sudden collapse into chaos. Vaughan's normalisation of deviance describes the mechanism: anomalies are reclassified as acceptable variations, one at a time, until the system is operating far outside its design envelope without anyone having noticed. And Sidney Dekker, in Drift into Failure (2011), generalises the point: complex systems drift towards failure through a series of locally rational decisions, each of which makes sense at the time, none of which is an error, and all of which together lead to catastrophe. In such systems the phrase errors become exceptions is not a description of a mature process. It is a description of drift.

What the plateau becomes. Territorialise — lightly. Deleuze and Guattari's concept of territory, developed in the plateau they title Of the Refrain, begins with a child singing in the dark to calm herself. The song creates a small zone of order — a territory — in the midst of chaos. Birds mark territories by song; people mark them by habits, routines, rituals. A territory is necessary: without it there is only chaos. But a territory is also always open — the refrain can be varied, the territory can be left, there is always a door through which one can go out.

The embedding plateau therefore keeps what is right about embedding. People need stable routines; they need to know what to do; they cannot reinvent their work every morning. The new practice must become a refrain — something repeated, reliable, a source of security. But the territory is built lightly: with explicit triggers for review (a volume of exceptions, a change in the customer base, a regulatory development, a pattern in complaints), with named exits (the conditions under which the practice would be suspended or replaced), and with a standing expectation that variation will be noticed and discussed rather than suppressed.

Above all, errors become exceptions is dropped and replaced by its opposite: errors become signals. Every variance is triaged (Figure 5): is it a breach of a molar line, to be corrected? A movement of the molecular line, to be discussed and perhaps adopted? Or a line of flight, something new, to be followed? The refrain holds; and it listens.

Keeps stable routines that people can rely on. Drops errors become exceptions. Adds review triggers, named exits, and the triage of every variance.

4.9 Post-embedding Continuous Improvement → Follow the lines of flight

What the bar says. Test · Assure · Feedback · Remediate · Assess materiality · Evolve techniques.

What it takes for granted. That improvement is a phase which follows embedding; that the process, once embedded, is improved incrementally by testing, assurance and feedback; that issues are remediated, assessed for materiality, and used to evolve technique. All of this is sound. What is taken for granted is the position: improvement comes after, as a band beneath the wheel, outside the cycle of change itself.

What complexity knows. In a far-from-equilibrium system there is no after. The system is always changing, and the fluctuations from which its next form will emerge are present from the start. Deleuze and Guattari's concept of the line of flight names these: the movements by which something escapes the existing organisation — not necessarily towards liberation, sometimes towards destruction, but always towards something new. Lines of flight are not added to a system after it has been organised; they are present in it from the beginning, and every organisation is in a sense an attempt to capture or block them.

What the plateau becomes. Follow the lines of flight. Improvement moves upstream: it is present from day one, as a standing capacity to notice what is escaping and to follow it. The six items of the old bar survive, but each is transformed into a standing instrument (Figure 5): Test becomes the probe log; Assure becomes the line register; Feedback becomes the consent record; Remediate becomes the correction log; Assess materiality becomes line calibration; Evolve techniques becomes the signal register. Chapter 12 describes each in detail.

The phrase follow, don't suppress needs one qualification, which Deleuze and Guattari make themselves. Not every line of flight is benign. Some lead towards what they call black holes — collapse, self-destruction, the turning of an escape into an abolition. A fraud is a line of flight; so is a rogue trader. Following a line of flight does not mean endorsing it. It means paying attention to it, understanding where it is going, and deciding — with the triage of Figure 5 — whether to amplify it, dampen it, or, if it crosses a molar line, stop it. What it rules out is the wheel's default: not noticing it at all, because it has been filed as an exception.

Keeps assurance and remediation. Drops improvement only after closure. Adds a signal register, open from the start, and the discipline of following before judging.

4.10 What survives, what goes, what is added

Read across the eight transformations, a pattern emerges. Nothing that the wheel does is discarded. Looking, designing, challenging, mapping, testing, agreeing, embedding and improving are all still there. What is discarded is a set of beliefs about those activities: that there is a current state, a right solution, a causal chain, an original to be copied, a convergent test, a moment of legitimacy, an exception, an after. And what is added is a set of capacities the wheel did not have: entry from anywhere, composition of heterogeneous elements, reading of lines, open mapping, parallel probing, renewable consent, light territory, and attention to flight.

The Element Concordance (Figure 4) completes the account by showing what has happened to every other element of the original — headline, strapline, centre, version, virtues, numbering, arrows, background. Its aim is simple: nothing in the original has been left silently behind.

5Six Principles of the Rhizome

Deleuze and Guattari give the rhizome six approximate characteristics. They are not axioms; the authors call them principles only for convenience, and they are numbered in a way that already resists counting — the first two are given together, as are the last two. This chapter takes each in turn and asks what it means for an organisation that must govern itself and be governed. Figure 3 is the principles drawn.

5.1 Principles one and two: connection and heterogeneity

Any point of a rhizome can be connected to any other, and must be. This is the first principle, and it is the sharpest break with the tree, which fixes an order by plotting a point and then descending from it. The second follows at once: the connections are heterogeneous. A rhizome does not link like with like. It links semiotic chains to organisations of power, to biological circumstances, to economic flows, to political struggles. Deleuze and Guattari's example is language itself: there is no language in itself, no universal grammar, only a throng of dialects, slangs, jargons and special languages, tied at every point to the bodies, institutions and powers that speak them.

For a regulated firm the first principle is a statement of fact before it is a prescription. Everything in the firm is already connected to everything else, often in ways no chart records. The complaint handler's script is connected to the product design team's assumptions about the target customer; the target customer is connected to the credit model; the credit model is connected to the capital calculation; the capital calculation is connected to the bonus pool; the bonus pool is connected to the sales team's behaviour; the sales team's behaviour is connected, through the customers it sells to, back to the complaint handler's queue. The arborescent organisation represents these connections only through common ancestors — two boxes that both report, eventually, to the chief executive. The rhizomatic practice draws them directly.

The second principle matters even more, because it names the thing that most governance frameworks quietly discard. Policies are about rules; risk registers about risks; process maps about steps; HR systems about people; IT inventories about systems. Each instrument is homogeneous: it collects like with like. But nothing that matters in a firm is produced by a homogeneous collection. A fair outcome for a vulnerable customer is produced by a rule, a script, a system screen, an agent's judgement, her training, her workload that afternoon, the customer's ability to explain himself, the call-routing algorithm that brought them together, and the ten minutes the agent was allowed. Heterogeneity is not a complication to be abstracted away. It is the substance of the thing.

The counter-design honours these principles by changing the unit of analysis from the process to the assemblage (Chapter 6) and by insisting, in the composition plateau, that every element of a change be considered together. On the diagram it is visible in the twenty-eight links between eight nodes, and in the fact that the name of the methodology — the most abstract, semiotic element — is wired into the same web as the most concrete practices.

5.2 Principle three: multiplicity

The third principle is that the multiple must be treated as a substantive — a multiplicity — rather than as a plurality of something that is fundamentally one. A multiplicity has neither subject nor object, only determinations, magnitudes and dimensions that cannot increase in number without the multiplicity changing in nature. A puppet's strings, Deleuze and Guattari observe, are not tied to the will of a puppeteer but to a multiplicity of nerve fibres that form another puppet in other dimensions connected to the first.

This is the source of the counter-design's version number. Deleuze and Guattari say that the multiple must be made by subtracting the unifying One from it — writing at n − 1 dimensions. The phrase is an instruction against the reflex of every organisation to find, behind the plurality of its activities, a single thing that they are all expressions of: the strategy, the culture, the purpose, the methodology. The reflex is understandable. A single unifying thing is easy to communicate and gives the comforting sense that everything is under one direction. But it is false, and its falsehood has consequences. When the firm believes its many cultures are expressions of one culture, it cannot see the local cultures that actually govern behaviour. When it believes its many processes implement one methodology, it cannot see the variety of methods actually in use.

Version n − 1 therefore means two things. It means that the counter-design never reaches a numbered release that closes it — there is no 2.0 waiting to supersede 1.0, only continual re-composition. And it means that the methodology subtracts itself from the multiplicity it serves: it does not claim to be the One behind the many plateaus but sits among them as one node more. That is what the gold name-card in Figure 3 announces: no centre — this too is a node.

Scholium 4

The mathematical root of multiplicity is Bernhard Riemann's concept of a manifold — a space defined not from outside, by embedding it in a larger space, but from inside, by the relations among its own dimensions. Deleuze took the idea through Bergson, who distinguished quantitative multiplicities (which can be divided without changing in kind) from qualitative ones (which change in kind whenever they are divided). A process map is a quantitative multiplicity: break it into sub-processes and each is still a process. An organisational culture is a qualitative multiplicity: break it into departments and what you get are not smaller pieces of the same culture but different cultures. Governance tools built for the first kind fail when applied to the second.

5.3 Principle four: asignifying rupture

A rhizome may be broken, shattered at a given spot, but it will start up again on one of its old lines, or on new lines. You can never get rid of ants, Deleuze and Guattari remark, because they form an animal rhizome that can rebound time and again after most of it has been destroyed. Every rhizome contains lines of segmentarity by which it is stratified, territorialised, organised, signified; but also lines of deterritorialisation down which it constantly flees. There is a rupture in the rhizome whenever segmentary lines explode into a line of flight — but the line of flight is part of the rhizome, and the lines are always tying back to one another.

The word asignifying is the key. A signifying rupture is one that means something within an existing system of meaning: the break is read as a failure, a deviation, a symptom, and is interpreted back into the structure it broke from. An asignifying rupture is one that is not interpreted in this way — that is allowed to be what it is, a break from which something else may grow, rather than an error to be explained.

The wheel's phrase errors become exceptions is the very model of a signifying rupture. Every break in the embedded process is immediately given a meaning — it is an exception — and that meaning reinscribes it in the structure: exceptions are counted, reported, closed. The counter-design's alternative is not to stop interpreting breaks; it is to delay the interpretation long enough to see what is growing at the point of breakage. The triage of Figure 5 asks what kind of line a variance belongs to before deciding what it means. Some ruptures will turn out to be breaches; some will be adaptations; some will be the first shoots of something nobody has yet named.

The principle has a further consequence, which regulators have arrived at by their own route. Operational resilience, as the UK authorities formulated it, starts from the premise that severe but plausible disruption will happen and asks firms to show that their important business services can recover within tolerance. That is a regulatory version of the fourth principle: the system will be broken; what matters is whether and how it starts up again. A firm that has mapped only its designed processes will not know how it recovers, because recovery happens along lines the design did not draw — the workaround, the manual fallback, the informal network of people who know how to get things done when the system is down. Those lines are the rhizome. Resilience is the rhizome's capacity to start up again.

RESILIENCE IS NOT THE ABSENCE OF RUPTURE. IT IS THE PRESENCE OF OTHER LINES.

5.4 Principles five and six: cartography and decalcomania

The last two principles are given together, and they are the ones on which the fourth plateau of the counter-design — Map, don't trace — rests. A rhizome is not amenable to any structural or generative model. It is a stranger to the idea of a genetic axis or a deep structure. Those models are tracings: they reproduce something given in advance, as a child traces a picture by laying paper over it. The rhizome is a map. The map does not reproduce an unconscious closed upon itself; it constructs the unconscious. It fosters connections between fields. It is itself part of the rhizome.

Deleuze and Guattari list the map's properties: it is open and connectable in all its dimensions; detachable, reversible, susceptible to constant modification; it can be torn, reversed, adapted to any kind of mounting, reworked by an individual, group or social formation. It can be drawn on a wall, conceived as a work of art, constructed as a political action or as a meditation. Above all, a map has multiple entryways, whereas a tracing always comes back to the same.

And then — this is easily missed — they reverse the hierarchy one more time. It is not enough to oppose maps to tracings as good to bad. One must always put the tracing back on the map. Tracings are useful: they show where a map has hardened, where a rhizome has formed a knot of arborescence, where a line of flight has been blocked. The danger of a tracing is not that it exists but that it is mistaken for the map — that a picture of what has already been organised is taken as a picture of everything that could be.

For process mapping the lesson is precise. The swim-lane diagram is a tracing, and it is useful: it shows how a process has been organised, who hands off to whom, where the controls sit. The error is to treat it as the territory. Put the tracing back on the map — lay it over a richer picture of how the work is actually done, where it varies, who works around what, where the people who know how to recover the service sit — and the tracing becomes legible as what it is: one hardened pathway through a larger and more mobile field.

5.5 What the principles do not license

Every concept that frees can also be abused, and Deleuze and Guattari are more careful about this than most of their readers. The rhizome is not a programme. It does not tell anyone what to do. It is not an argument for abolishing hierarchy, for flattening organisations, for removing controls, for letting a thousand flowers bloom. Its authors explicitly warn that rhizomes carry micro-fascisms as easily as they carry liberations, that lines of flight can turn into lines of destruction, and that the most supple segmentations can be more oppressive than rigid ones.

The practical translation for governance is a set of things the counter-design does not claim. It does not claim that every connection is good: some connections are conflicts of interest, information leaks, collusion. It does not claim that heterogeneity is always a strength: some mixtures are simply confusion. It does not claim that every rupture should be allowed to grow: some are frauds. It does not claim that maps are always better than tracings: in a payment authorisation chain, a tracing that is followed exactly is precisely what is wanted.

What it claims is narrower. It claims that a methodology which sees only trees will mistake the territory for its tracing, will filter out lateral signals, will reduce heterogeneous outcomes to homogeneous components, will force qualitative multiplicities into quantitative categories, will interpret every rupture as an exception and will draw maps that cannot be torn. And it claims that a methodology which also sees the rhizome — which reads the six principles in its own operations — will see more, sooner, and govern better. Chapter 13 returns to the abuses in detail.

5.6 The principles as a diagnostic

The six principles can be turned into six questions, which any governance team can ask of any change, policy or control.

  • Connection. What is this connected to that our documentation does not show? Who would notice first if it went wrong, and are they connected to the people who could act?
  • Heterogeneity. What non-procedural elements — systems, incentives, moods, workloads, relationships — does this depend on? Which of them have we designed, and which have we assumed?
  • Multiplicity. Where are we treating a plurality as if it were one thing? Whose version of the process, the culture, the customer are we working from?
  • Asignifying rupture. When this breaks, how will it start up again? Along which lines? Have we mapped them? When something deviates, do we interpret it before we have looked at it?
  • Cartography. Is our picture of this a map or a tracing? Can it be torn, revised, entered from several directions, drawn by the people who do the work?
  • Decalcomania. Where have we laid our tracings back over the map? Where might we have mistaken one for the other?

These questions do not replace the disciplines of the wheel. They sit beside them, and they ask what the disciplines alone cannot see.

6Assemblage Theory for the Regulated Firm

If the rhizome describes the shape of the counter-design, the assemblage describes its substance. This chapter sets out what an assemblage is — in Deleuze and Guattari, and in the more systematic reconstruction offered by Manuel DeLanda — and then reads a regulated firm, a lending process and a control as assemblages. The aim is to show that the concept is not decorative. It changes what one looks at, and therefore what one can govern.

6.1 Agencement

The English word assemblage translates the French agencement, and something is lost in the translation. Agencement carries the sense of an arrangement, a layout, a fitting-together — and also, through its root, of agency: an arrangement that does something. An assemblage is not a heap. It is a working arrangement of heterogeneous elements whose operation produces effects none of the elements could produce alone.

Deleuze and Guattari describe every assemblage as having two sides and two axes. On one side it is a machinic assemblage of bodies, actions and passions — an intermingling of physical things reacting to one another. On the other it is a collective assemblage of enunciation, of acts and statements — the incorporeal transformations by which words change the status of bodies. A judge's sentence transforms an accused into a convict without touching him; a credit decision transforms an applicant into a borrower; a sign-off transforms a draft into a policy. Bodies and statements are not separate realms. They are two faces of the same arrangement, each acting on the other.

Along one axis an assemblage has territorial sides, which stabilise it, and cutting edges of deterritorialisation, which carry it away. Every assemblage is both: a holding-together and a coming-apart.

6.2 DeLanda's reconstruction

Manuel DeLanda, in A New Philosophy of Society (2006) and Assemblage Theory (2016), took these scattered remarks and made of them something close to a social ontology. Three of his moves are especially useful for governance.

Relations of exteriority. DeLanda distinguishes assemblages from organic totalities. In a totality, the parts are defined by their relations to the whole — a heart is a heart only within a body — and cannot be removed without ceasing to be what they are. In an assemblage, the parts are related by relations of exteriority: they can be detached from one assemblage and plugged into another without losing their identity, and their properties are not exhausted by any particular set of relations. A credit analyst can move from the mortgage team to the commercial team and remain a credit analyst; a scoring model can be redeployed; a policy can be copied to a subsidiary. This is why assemblages can be recomposed, and why change is possible at all.

Properties and capacities. DeLanda distinguishes a component's properties — what it is, which are given — from its capacities — what it can do, which depend on what it interacts with and are therefore not given in advance. A knife has the property of sharpness and the capacity to cut, but only in interaction with something cuttable. Capacities are real but not actual until exercised, and they are potentially infinite, because the number of things a component might interact with is open. This is Spinoza's question — what can a body do? — given an analytical form. It is also exactly the question that a process design does not ask. A design specifies properties: this role does this step; this system holds this data. It does not ask what capacities will emerge when the role, the system, the customer and the afternoon's workload meet.

Emergence without reduction. The properties of an assemblage are emergent: they arise from the interactions of its components and are not possessed by any of them. But because the components remain separable, the whole is not an irreducible mystery either. One can explain an assemblage's properties by the mechanisms of interaction among its parts — and one can change the whole by changing a part or a relation. Assemblage theory is thus neither reductionist (the whole is the sum of the parts) nor holist (the whole is prior to the parts). It is the ontology complexity science has always needed.

6.3 The firm as an assemblage of assemblages

DeLanda insists that assemblages are nested: people form networks, networks form organisations, organisations form markets and governments, and at each level the entities are assemblages whose components are themselves assemblages. There is no fundamental level. A regulated firm is an assemblage of divisions, which are assemblages of teams, which are assemblages of people, routines, systems and documents; and it is itself a component in larger assemblages — the market it competes in, the regulatory system that supervises it, the economy it lends into.

This has an immediate consequence for the question that every regulator now asks: does the firm have the culture it claims? On an arborescent view, culture is a property of the whole that cascades down — set the tone at the top and it will flow to the branches. On the assemblage view, culture is an emergent property of interactions at every level, and the culture that matters for a customer's outcome is the one produced in the assemblage she actually meets — this team, this script, this system, this manager's way of reviewing calls. Tone at the top is a component of that assemblage, sometimes an important one. It is never the whole of it, and it is never sufficient.

The Financial Conduct Authority's long insistence that it judges culture by what firms do rather than what they say is, read this way, an assemblage-theoretic position. It refuses to infer the properties of the whole from the statements of one component.

6.4 A lending decision, read as an assemblage

Take a single mortgage application and list what actually produces the decision. There is the applicant, with her income, her history, her ability to understand the questions and her anxiety about the answer. There is the broker who brought her, with his commission structure and his sense of which lender will say yes. There is the online form, with its fields and drop-down menus, which can only record what it was built to record. There is the credit reference data, with its own gaps and lags. There is the scoring model, trained on a past that may not resemble the present. There is the affordability calculator, with stress rates set by policy and amended by the regulator. There is the valuation, produced by a surveyor working from comparable sales, a site visit or a desktop model, under time pressure, with professional standards and professional indemnity in the background. There is the underwriter, with her mandate, her queue, her experience, her training, her manager's view of acceptable turnaround, and her intuition about cases that look wrong. There is the lending policy, the risk appetite, the concentration limits, the conduct rules, the Consumer Duty's demand for good outcomes and fair value. There is the funding cost that morning.

The decision is produced by all of these together. No process map shows them all; most show the underwriter's steps and the system's checks. And the outcome's quality — whether the borrower can afford the loan through a rate rise, whether the valuation will hold in a downturn, whether the product was right for her — is an emergent property of the assemblage, not of any component.

The wheel's second stage would have the practitioner design the right process solution for this. The counter-design asks her to compose the assemblage: to look at every component and every relation, to ask what each can do in combination with the others, and to try several compositions. It may be that the most effective change is not a new process step at all but a change in the broker's commission structure, or an extra field on the form, or ten more minutes in the underwriter's target time, or a desk note telling surveyors which kinds of property have recently been over-valued. Those are not process changes. They are assemblage changes. A process methodology cannot see them; an assemblage practice is built to.

Scholium 5

For readers who work in recoveries and receivership, the assemblage view will be familiar from practice rather than theory. A distressed property loan is never just a loan in default. It is an assemblage of the asset and its physical condition, the tenants and their leases, the borrower and his other creditors, the security documentation and its quirks, the valuation and its assumptions, the local market, the lender's provisioning position and appetite for loss, the receiver's powers under the charge and under statute, the professional duties owed to the borrower and to the lender, and the timetable that all of these impose. The art of a workout is precisely to recompose that assemblage — to find which component or relation can be changed to release value — rather than to follow a recovery process step by step. 6.5 A control, read as an assemblage

Now take something governance professionals usually treat as a simple object: a control. Say, the four-eyes check on high-value payments. In the control library it has properties — an owner, a frequency, a design description, a test script, a last test result. In practice it is an assemblage: the first operator who enters the payment, the second who approves it, the screen that shows the second operator what she needs to see (or does not), the volume of payments in the queue, the time of day, the relationship between the two operators, the training they had, the culture of their team about whether one queries a colleague's work, the escalation route if something looks wrong, and the system log that records that both pressed the button.

A control test will typically sample the log and confirm that two different user IDs approved each payment. That tests one property of one component. It does not test the capacity of the assemblage to catch an error — which depends on whether the second operator actually looks, which depends on the queue, the screen, the relationship, the culture. Michael Power's The Audit Society (1997) describes exactly this problem: audit tends to verify the auditable — the log, the signature, the evidence of procedure — rather than the thing the procedure is meant to secure. Power called the result rituals of verification. They produce comfort without necessarily producing control.

The assemblage view does not abolish control testing. It asks that the test be directed at capacities as well as properties: not only did two people approve? but would this arrangement catch the kind of error it exists to catch, under the conditions it actually operates in? Answering that question requires looking at the whole assemblage, which is why the line register (Chapter 12) records not only which controls exist but what they depend on.

6.6 Territorialisation and coding

DeLanda's second axis — territorialisation and deterritorialisation — gives a vocabulary for the stability and instability of assemblages that governance badly needs.

An assemblage is territorialised when its boundaries are sharpened and its internal homogeneity increased: when a team is given a clear remit, a clear location, a clear membership; when a process is standardised; when a policy is enforced uniformly. Territorialisation produces stability, identity, predictability. It is what the wheel's embedding stage does.

An assemblage is deterritorialised when its boundaries blur and its internal heterogeneity increases: when a team works across locations, borrows people from elsewhere, adopts tools nobody issued; when a process varies by branch; when a new technology lets people bypass established routes. Deterritorialisation produces flexibility, innovation, and also risk.

DeLanda adds a third parameter, specific to assemblages that involve language and rules: coding. An assemblage is highly coded when its behaviour is specified by explicit rules — procedures, regulations, contracts. It is decoded when those rules are loosened or ignored and behaviour is governed by tacit norms, judgement and negotiation.

A regulated firm is a field in which these parameters are constantly in tension. Regulation pushes towards territorialisation and coding: clear accountabilities, documented procedures, consistent outcomes. Markets, technology and customers push towards deterritorialisation and decoding: new channels, new products, new behaviours that no rule anticipated. The wheel resolves the tension in one direction only: every change ends in embedding, which is to say in territorialisation and coding. The counter-design asks that the parameters be set, deliberately and differently for different lines: high territorialisation and coding on the molar lines that the law requires, lower on the molecular lines where judgement serves better, and attention to the deterritorialising edges where lines of flight are forming. That is what Territorialise — lightly means in practice.

6.7 Why the concept earns its place

It would be fair to ask whether all this vocabulary is needed. Could one not simply say that outcomes depend on many factors, that controls should be tested for effectiveness, that organisations need both stability and flexibility? One could, and governance texts often do. What assemblage theory adds is a way of thinking those platitudes together, so that they produce different practice.

It makes heterogeneity the default rather than an afterthought, so that the first question about any change is what is it made of? It makes capacities, not properties, the object of design and assurance, so that the question is what can it do? It makes recomposition possible by insisting that parts survive their relations, so that the question becomes which part or relation could we change? And it gives a precise vocabulary — territorialisation, coding, and their opposites — for the deliberate setting of stability and flexibility line by line, rather than the blanket choice between control and empowerment that dominates most change debates.

7The Eight Plateaus, I: Sensing and Composing

Chapter 4 showed how each stage of the wheel becomes a plateau. This chapter and the next go inside the plateaus: what it is like to work on each, what practices sustain it, what it produces, how it connects to the others, and how it typically goes wrong. The first four plateaus — Enter in the middle, Compose the assemblage, Read the lines, Map, don't trace — are the plateaus of sensing and composing. The second four, in Chapter 8, are the plateaus of acting and holding.

7.1 Working on a plateau

Before the plateaus themselves, a word on what it means to work on one. A stage is entered, completed and left; its output is handed to the next. A plateau is sustained. A GRC function working rhizomatically does not run its plateaus in turn but keeps all eight alive at different intensities: a low, continuous hum of sensing, mapping and line-reading across the whole firm, rising to high intensity on whichever plateaus a particular change demands. One way to picture it is as a mixing desk rather than a sequence: eight channels, all open, each turned up or down as the situation requires.

This has a resourcing implication that is easy to miss. In a stage-based methodology, people are allocated to phases: the discovery team, the design team, the testing team. In a plateau-based practice, capacities are distributed: every team has some capacity to sense, to map, to read lines, to probe, and specialists support them where intensity rises. The Three Lines Model already gestures at this — management owns risk, the second line supports and challenges — but in practice the second line often becomes a stage-gate. The plateaus ask it to become a set of standing capacities instead.

7.2 Enter in the middle

The intensity. This plateau is the work of attention: noticing what is already moving before deciding what to move. Its characteristic mood is curiosity held against the pressure to act. Its enemy is the baseline — the document that purports to describe the current state and thereby licenses everyone to stop looking.

Practices. Three practices sustain it. The first is distributed sensing: standing channels through which the firm hears about itself continuously — complaint text analysed for emerging themes rather than only counted; frontline huddles that end with the question what surprised you this week?; exit interviews read for patterns; regulators' thematic findings read as signals from the wider system. Snowden's narrative-capture methods, in which large numbers of people tell short stories about their experience and index them themselves, are a powerful version of this: they let patterns emerge from the material without an analyst imposing categories first.

The second is situated recording. Every sensemaking note records where it was made from: whose account, gathered how, by whom, with what position in the firm. This is not bureaucracy. It is the minimum honesty required by the recognition, developed in earlier work on the Stacey matrix, that there is no view from nowhere. A note that says the current state is X conceals its vantage. A note that says from the complaints team's queue, over three weeks, it looks as if X invites someone from elsewhere to say what it looks like from there.

The third is tendency-reading. Jullien's account of shì — the configuration of a situation and the propensity it carries — offers a discipline the West lacks a word for. Before asking what we want to achieve, ask what this situation is already tending towards. Where is energy already flowing? Who is already adapting, and in which direction? Which way would things go if nobody intervened? A good change works with these propensities where it can and redirects them where it must. A bad change ignores them and is surprised when the situation resists.

Outputs. A dated sensemaking note with its vantage recorded; a short list of tendencies in motion; the names of people already adapting (who are often the best guides to what is possible).

Connections. In Figure 3 this plateau links most densely to Read the lines, Experiment in parallel and Territorialise lightly — sensing feeds line-reading, suggests probes, and keeps watch on whether embedded routines are still fitting.

Failure mode. Treating the first account heard as the current state; or, at the other extreme, sensing forever and never acting. The plateau is sustained, but it is not a substitute for the others.

7.3 Compose the assemblage

The intensity. The work of arrangement: seeing all the components that will produce an outcome and trying out ways of fitting them together. Its mood is that of the bricoleur rather than the engineer — Lévi-Strauss's distinction, which Deleuze and Guattari use: the engineer designs from a plan with purpose-made parts; the bricoleur works with what is to hand, repurposing it. Most organisational change is bricolage pretending to be engineering.

Practices. The first is the component inventory: for a given outcome, list every element that will produce it, across all the heterogeneous categories — people and their skills; roles and their mandates; systems and their screens; data and its quality; rules, policies and scripts; incentives and targets; documents and records; customers and their capacities; time and its pressures; moods and relationships. The inventory is always incomplete, and that is fine; the point is to break the habit of seeing only procedure.

The second is capacity questioning. For each component and each relation, ask not what it is but what it can do in this arrangement. What can this screen let an operator notice? What can this target make a team ignore? What can this customer explain, and what will she be unable to? This is DeLanda's distinction between properties and capacities put to work.

The third is composition sketching: drafting two or three alternative arrangements, each of which might produce the outcome, and noting what each is expected to be capable of. The sketches are not detailed designs. They are hypotheses about assemblages, to be tested on the next plateau.

The fourth, and the one that makes this plateau compatible with regulation, is constraint-first framing. Before any sketching, draw the molar lines: which legal and regulatory requirements, which prescribed responsibilities, which risk limits bound the space? These are not inputs to be traded off. They are the walls of the room in which composition happens.

Outputs. A component inventory; a composition sketch per option; the molar constraints drawn as the boundary.

Connections. Composition is bound to Enunciate together by a gold, molar link in Figure 3, because consent must cover the whole composition, not just the procedural slice of it. It links by molecular lines to Experiment in parallel (where the sketches are tried) and to Map, don't trace (where the existing assemblage is understood).

Failure mode. Collapsing back into process design — producing a composition sketch that turns out, on inspection, to be a swim-lane diagram with a few extra boxes.

7.4 Read the lines

The intensity. Discrimination: telling apart the rigid, the supple and the escaping. Its mood is that of the auditor at her best — sceptical, exact, unwilling to accept a classification because it is convenient — turned not on compliance with a rule but on whether the rules are drawn in the right places.

Practices. Chapter 10 develops the theory of the three lines; here the concern is how to read them. The first practice is the line walk: taking a process, a service or a change and going through its components asking, of each, three questions. Is this held rigid, and if so by what — law, contract, accountability, physics? Does this flex in practice, and if so how much, and who decides? Is anything here escaping — not fitting any category, appearing where it should not, being used for something it was not meant for?

The second is contested-line review. Independent challenge — the second line, internal audit, a non-executive, a peer from another division — is directed specifically at the places where the classification is uncertain or disputed. Should this check be rigid, or is it rigid only because nobody has dared to relax it? Should this practice be allowed to flex, or has it flexed across a line that must be held? These are the questions where independent challenge adds most value, because the people inside the situation have the strongest reasons to classify in their own favour.

The third is drift detection. Vaughan's normalisation of deviance happens when a line of flight is quietly reclassified as part of the molecular line of ordinary practice — when an anomaly becomes the way we do things without anyone deciding it should. The practice is to compare line readings over time: what was recorded as an anomaly six months ago and is now recorded as normal practice? Who decided? Was it decided at all?

Outputs. Line register entries for the change (Chapter 12); a short list of contested lines with the challenge recorded; any drift identified.

Connections. Read the lines links in Figure 3 to Enter in the middle (sensing supplies the material), to Enunciate together (consent needs to know what is rigid and what is negotiable), and to Follow the lines of flight (escapes identified here are followed there).

Failure mode. Reading every line as molar. This is the regulated firm's characteristic error: under pressure from supervisors and fear of personal accountability, everything is made rigid, every practice becomes a control, and the result is a firm with a control for everything and attention for nothing. The opposite error — reading everything as molecular and negotiable — is rarer in regulated firms but catastrophic when it happens.

7.5 Map, don't trace

The intensity. Drawing as intervention. Its mood is that of the field geographer rather than the land registry clerk: interested in what is there, how it moves, where the paths are, and conscious that the act of drawing will change how people move.

Practices. The first is drawing with. Maps are made in the room with the people who do the work, on the wall, in their words, with their disagreements left visible. Patricia Shaw's account of organisational change as conversation is relevant here: the map is not a deliverable produced by analysts but a conversation made visible, and its value lies as much in the conversation as in the drawing.

The second is showing variation. Where a tracing shows the norm, a map shows the range: the path most cases take, and also the paths some cases take, and why. Hollnagel's insistence that variability is the source of success as well as failure becomes, on the map, a set of marked places where the work flexes, with a note of what it flexes in response to.

The third is marking recovery lines. Every map should show not only how the work normally goes but how it starts up again when it breaks: the manual fallback, the person who knows the workaround, the informal channel used when the system is down. These are the rhizome's lines of asignifying rupture, and they are where operational resilience actually lives.

The fourth is dating and signing. Every map carries its date, its authors and its vantage, and is treated as a version, not as the truth. When it is revised, the old version is kept, because the difference between versions is itself information about how the work is moving.

The fifth, which follows Deleuze and Guattari's own reversal, is putting the tracing back on the map. The formal process diagram is not discarded. It is laid over the richer map so that everyone can see which part of the field it covers, where it has hardened usefully, and where it misrepresents.

Outputs. A living map with version history; marked variation points; marked recovery lines; the formal tracing overlaid.

Connections. In Figure 3, Map, don't trace connects to Compose the assemblage (understanding the existing assemblage), to Enunciate together (the map is a basis for consent), to Territorialise lightly (maps show where routines are holding and where they are not) and to the name-node — maps are one of the main ways the practice describes itself.

Failure mode. A tracing in a map's clothing: the participatory workshop whose output is validated, signed off and frozen as procedure. James C. Scott's warning applies: the most dangerous maps are those that make a territory legible to administrators at the cost of destroying the local knowledge that made it work.

Scholium 6

Scott's concept of mētis — the practical, local, experiential knowledge that cannot be fully codified — is worth dwelling on, because governance professionals spend much of their time trying to codify it. Scott's examples range from Prussian scientific forestry, whose neat monocultures collapsed within a few generations, to Soviet collective farms and planned cities like Brasília. In each case the state's simplified map replaced a complex, adaptive practice and destroyed its resilience. The governance lesson is not that codification is wrong — molar lines must be written down — but that every codification has a cost in mētis, and the cost should be counted. A map, in Deleuze and Guattari's sense, is a way of representing work that preserves rather than replaces the mētis it describes.

7.6 The four together

The four sensing-and-composing plateaus form a cluster in Figure 3, densely interlinked, in the upper half of the diagram. That position is not accidental. They correspond to the wheel's first four stages — which in the wheel's own clockwise layout also occupy its upper and left side — and they share a character: they are the plateaus on which the practice learns what it is dealing with. In a stage-based methodology, learning is front-loaded: discover, design, review, map, then act. On the plateaus, learning never stops. The sensing plateaus continue at a low hum while the acting plateaus run, and rise again in intensity whenever something on the acting plateaus surprises.

That continuity is the single most important practical difference between the wheel and the assemblage. The wheel learns, then acts. The assemblage acts while learning, and learns from acting.

8The Eight Plateaus, II: Acting and Holding

The second cluster of plateaus — Experiment in parallel, Enunciate together, Territorialise lightly, Follow the lines of flight — is where the assemblage acts on itself, holds what works and keeps watch on what escapes. In Figure 3 they occupy the lower half of the diagram, and they include both of the gold molar links: this is where the practice touches the rigid obligations a regulated firm cannot negotiate.

8.1 Experiment in parallel

The intensity. Disciplined trial. The mood is that of the naturalist rather than the engineer: interested in what happens, prepared to be surprised, unwilling to defend a hypothesis against the evidence. Its enemy is the flagship pilot — the single, well-funded, much-announced trial that cannot be allowed to fail.

Practices. The first practice is portfolio design. For a given change, several probes are designed together, deliberately different: one that tests the composition the team favours, one that tests an alternative, one that tests an assumption everyone shares, sometimes one designed to fail in an informative way. Snowden's guidance is that the portfolio should include oblique and even contradictory probes, because in a complex system you do not know which will be amplified, and a portfolio of similar probes tells you only about one region of the possibility space.

The second is bounding. Every probe is bounded by the molar lines. Before it starts, the team records which rigid obligations it touches and how they are protected: customer outcomes monitored, a human review of any adverse decision, a capped scale, a stop condition. A probe that cannot be bounded in this way is not safe-to-fail and does not run.

The third is stating signals in advance. For each probe, what would count as a sign that it is taking — that the system is amplifying it? What would count as a sign that it is harming — that it should be dampened or stopped? Stating these in advance does not prevent surprise; it prevents the retrospective rationalisation by which any result can be read as success.

The fourth is amplify and dampen. As probes run, energy is shifted: more resource, more scope, more attention to probes that are producing patterns worth having; less to those that are not. This is not a single go/no-go decision at the end. It is continuous, and it is recorded in the probe log, so that the history of what was tried and what the system did with it accumulates as organisational learning.

Outputs. Probe log entries; amplify, dampen and stop decisions with reasons; a growing body of evidence about what the assemblage can do.

Connections. Experiment links in Figure 3 to Compose (whose sketches it tests), to Enter in the middle (which suggests what to probe), to Territorialise lightly (what takes is gradually embedded) and to Follow the lines of flight (probes often reveal escapes).

Failure mode. The disguised pilot: a single favoured design run alongside two token alternatives that nobody believes in. The test is whether anyone would be genuinely pleased if one of the alternatives won.

Scholium 7

Regulators have built something like this plateau into their own toolkits. Regulatory sandboxes, first developed by the UK's Financial Conduct Authority in 2016 and widely copied, allow firms to test innovative propositions with real customers under bounded conditions — limited scale, specific safeguards, close supervisory contact. A sandbox is a regulatory molar line deliberately set to permit a probe: rigid where customer protection demands it, relaxed where it does not. The concept shows that safe-to-fail experimentation and robust regulation are not opposites. They are compatible when the lines are drawn with care.

8.2 Enunciate together

The intensity. Collective utterance. The mood is deliberative: the practice of bringing the people whose consent determines whether a change will hold into the same conversation, and producing with them a statement they can all stand behind — or stand beside, with their reservations recorded.

Practices. The first is convening the right assemblage. Who must consent for this change to take hold? Not only the governance forum and the accountable executive, but the people who will do the work, the people affected by it downstream, and — where outcomes are at stake — some representation of customers, whether through research, complaints data, a vulnerable-customer specialist or a consumer panel. Deleuze and Guattari's collective assemblage of enunciation is the idea that a statement's force comes from the arrangement it is made in. A policy agreed by a committee whose members will not operate it has the force of a committee statement. A policy agreed by the people who will operate it has a different force.

The second is writing the statement. What exactly is being agreed? On what conditions? For how long, and what would trigger its review? What is held rigid in it and what is left to judgement? The consent record (Chapter 12) is the written form of this statement, and its drafting is itself part of the enunciation — disagreements that surface while drafting are the most useful thing the plateau produces.

The third is recording dissent. Dissent is not closed out by the decision. It is recorded, with its reasons, and kept. Two reasons justify this. First, dissent is very often the earliest visible form of a line of flight: the person who says this won't work for the customers I see may be seeing something nobody else has. Second, recorded dissent protects the accountable executive as much as anyone: it is evidence that challenge was invited, heard and weighed, which is the heart of what reasonable steps means.

The fourth is renewal. Consent decays. The statement carries its review triggers, and when they fire — a change in conditions, a pattern in complaints, a probe result, a regulatory development — the enunciation is renewed, which may mean confirmed, amended or withdrawn.

Outputs. A consent record with conditions, duration, triggers and dissent; evidence of reasonable steps for the accountable executive.

Connections. Enunciate together sits at the junction of both gold molar links in Figure 3 — from Compose and to Territorialise — because it is where rigid obligations are carried from design into practice. It also links to Map, don't trace, Read the lines, and the name-node.

Failure mode. The consent record as sign-off sheet: a form with signatures, completed once, filed, never reopened. The test is whether anyone has ever reopened one.

8.3 Territorialise — lightly

The intensity. The making of a refrain. The mood is domestic: making a place where people can work without reinventing their work every day. Deleuze and Guattari's plateau on the refrain has three moments: a child in the dark sings to make a centre of calm in chaos; she draws a circle around that centre to mark a home; and she opens the circle a crack to let someone in, or to go out herself. All three are needed. Without the first there is only chaos; without the second only a fragile point; without the third a prison.

Practices. The first is making the refrain: turning what the probes found and the enunciation agreed into a routine people can rely on — clear, rehearsed, supported by training, systems and documentation. This is the wheel's embedding, kept intact. People need it, and so does the firm: molar obligations require consistent execution.

The second is installing triggers: explicit, observable conditions under which the routine will be reviewed. Exception volumes above a threshold; a change in the customer mix; a new complaint theme; a regulatory publication; a technology change. The triggers are agreed in the enunciation and recorded in the consent record.

The third is naming exits: the conditions under which the routine would be suspended or replaced, and who has authority to do so. A refrain with no exit becomes, in Deleuze and Guattari's terms, a black hole — a territory that captures everything that comes near it.

The fourth is triage of variance. Every deviation from the routine is asked which line it belongs to (Figure 5). Molar breaches go to correction. Molecular drift goes to discussion and, if it serves customers and stays within the molar lines, may be adopted into the routine. Lines of flight go to the signal register. The routine holds; it also listens.

Outputs. An operating rhythm; a trigger list; an exit statement; triage decisions.

Connections. Territorialise is linked in gold from Enunciate together, because what is embedded must be what was consented to, and in molecular lines to Enter in the middle, Experiment and Map.

Failure mode. Forgetting the door. The refrain hardens into a routine that no longer hears variation, and the firm begins its slide towards Snowden's cliff.

8.4 Follow the lines of flight

The intensity. Vigilant curiosity. The mood is that of the scout, or of the naturalist who notices the bird that should not be here at this time of year. Its enemy is the exception report — the instrument by which anomalies are converted into counts and filed.

Practices. The first is the open register. From the first day of any change, the signal register is open: anyone can log something that does not fit, with a short note of what they saw. The bar to entry is deliberately low. A register that requires a root-cause analysis before logging will receive no entries.

The second is following before judging. A logged signal is not immediately classified as good or bad. Someone is asked to follow it: where else is this happening? What is it connected to? What is it a sign of? Following may take an hour or a month. The aim is to understand the line before deciding what to do with it.

The third is triage and decision. Once followed, the signal is triaged by line. Some turn out to be molar breaches and go to correction. Some are molecular drift and go to discussion. Some are genuinely new, and for those the decision is to amplify (design a probe around it), dampen (withdraw energy, monitor), or stop (if it threatens a molar line). The decision is recorded with an owner.

The fourth is periodic reading. The register is read as a whole, regularly, by people with enough distance to see patterns: the risk committee, the board, the regulator in supervisory meetings. Read this way, the signal register becomes the firm's peripheral vision — the place where emerging risks and opportunities first appear, before they have names or categories.

Outputs. Signal register entries with triage decisions and owners; pattern readings.

Connections. In Figure 3 this plateau sits in the lower right, linked to Read the lines, Experiment, Compose and the name-node, with its own line of flight running off the corner of the figure.

Failure mode. Two, symmetrical. The first is the wheel's: not noticing at all. The second is romanticism: treating every anomaly as a precious sign of emergence and refusing to stop anything. Deleuze and Guattari's warning about lines of flight that turn into lines of abolition is precise. A fraud is a line of flight. So is a rogue trader. So is a culture of mis-selling that begins as one team's clever interpretation of a target. Following is never endorsing.

8.5 The eight together: an illustration

It may help to see the eight plateaus working together on a single, deliberately ordinary example. A retail lender notices, through its complaint analytics, a small rise in complaints from customers who had arranged payment holidays and then found their credit files marked in ways they did not expect. Nobody designed this; nobody approved it; it is happening.

The work enters in the middle, at Follow the lines of flight: the signal is logged and followed. It turns out to be connected to a system change made months earlier for an unrelated reason, interacting with a reporting rule and a script the collections team uses. Already four other plateaus are lit. Read the lines: the credit-reporting obligation is molar; the script is molecular; the system interaction is a line of flight nobody had seen. Map, don't trace: the collections team draws, on a wall, how a payment holiday actually flows through their work, and the formal process diagram is laid over it — revealing that the diagram stops where the problem begins. Compose the assemblage: three compositions are sketched — a system fix, a script change with a manual check, a change in when customers are told what will be reported. Experiment in parallel: the script change and the customer-communication change are probed in two teams for three weeks, bounded by a manual review of every affected credit file; the system fix is scoped. Enunciate together: collections, the credit-reporting specialist, complaints, the accountable executive and a vulnerable-customer specialist agree the composition that worked, with a review trigger tied to complaint volumes and one dissent recorded about timing. Territorialise lightly: the new routine is trained and documented, with the trigger and an exit installed. And the signal register stays open, because the system interaction that started it all may be producing effects elsewhere.

Nothing in this account is exotic. Most experienced GRC teams would recognise every step. What the plateaus add is a way of seeing that the steps did not happen in order, did not start at discovery, did not end at embedding, and did not need to — and a set of instruments for doing them deliberately rather than by accident.

9No Centre: Governance without a Sovereign Hub

The most provocative feature of the counter-design is its empty centre. A regulated firm, a reader might object, cannot have no centre: the law requires a board, a chief executive, named senior managers, a management responsibilities map. This chapter answers the objection. It distinguishes the centre that the counter-design vacates — a centre of significance, through which everything must pass and about which everything turns — from the knots of accountability that it keeps. And it asks how a practice without a hub can hold the anxiety that hubs exist, in part, to contain.

9.1 Two meanings of centre

The word centre covers two different things, and the argument depends on keeping them apart.

The first is a centre of significance: a point from which meaning and direction flow, through which information must pass to count, and about which the system organises itself. The hub of the 2015 wheel is a centre in this sense. It holds the methodology's name and its virtues, and every stage on the rim is defined by its position relative to that hub. In Deleuze and Guattari's terms, arborescent systems have centres of significance and subjectification, and information travels along pre-established paths to and from them.

The second is a point of accountability: a place where responsibility for an outcome is fixed on a named person who must answer for it. The Senior Managers and Certification Regime creates many such points. Each senior manager holds prescribed responsibilities; each must take reasonable steps to prevent breaches in their area; each can be held personally to account.

The counter-design vacates the first and keeps the second. There is no hub about which the work turns, no single point through which all information must flow, no master methodology from which every practice derives. But there are many points of accountability — knots in the rhizome, held rigid, which the gold molar lines of Figure 3 connect. Accountability is distributed across the network, not concentrated at its centre. And that, on reflection, is what the SM&CR itself requires: not one accountable person at the top of a tree, but a map of named people across the firm, each answerable for their part.

9.2 Heterarchy and polycentricity

The idea of organisation without a single centre is older than Deleuze. In 1945 the neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch published a short paper on what he called a heterarchy of values, prompted by a puzzle about the brain. Rats in experiments showed preferences that were not transitive: preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A. No hierarchy of values could produce that; it implied an organisation in which different parts could take the lead in different circumstances. McCulloch called it heterarchy — rule by others, by whichever part was best placed to lead at the moment.

Elinor Ostrom, whose work on the governance of common-pool resources won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, developed a related idea at the scale of institutions: polycentric governance, in which many centres of decision-making, formally independent of one another, take account of each other in competitive and cooperative relationships and resolve conflicts through overlapping arrangements. Ostrom's fieldwork showed that polycentric systems — fisheries, irrigation schemes, forests managed by overlapping communities and authorities — often outperformed both centralised state management and pure markets, precisely because they matched decisions to the scale and knowledge of the people who had to live with them.

Stafford Beer's Viable System Model, developed from the 1950s and set out in Brain of the Firm (1972), gives a cybernetic version of the same insight. Beer argued that any viable system must contain, at every level of recursion, the same five functions — operations, coordination, control, intelligence and policy — and that viability depends on giving operational units as much autonomy as is compatible with the cohesion of the whole. His key principle was Ashby's law of requisite variety: only variety can absorb variety. A central controller with less variety than the system it controls will fail. The answer is not a bigger controller but distributed control, with the centre attending only to what cannot be handled locally.

These three traditions — heterarchy, polycentricity, viable systems — converge on the claim that the counter-design makes. A firm in a complex environment cannot be governed from a single centre, because no centre has requisite variety. It must be governed by many centres, each matched to its part of the environment, connected laterally, and coordinated without being commanded.

9.3 The SM&CR as a rhizome of knots

It is worth reading the SM&CR this way, because it is often read as the very opposite of a rhizome — as the strongest possible reassertion of arborescence after a crisis in which responsibility dissolved. In one sense it is. It insists on named individuals, clear allocation, no gaps and no overlaps. It is a tree in its insistence that every responsibility can be traced to a person.

But look at what it actually requires those persons to do. A senior manager must take reasonable steps to prevent a regulatory breach in her area. Reasonable steps cannot be discharged by signing a document at a gate. They require her to know what is happening in her area, to have arrangements that would surface problems, to respond to warnings, to challenge, to follow up. In a complex domain, they require exactly the practices of the plateaus: sensing, line-reading, mapping, probing, consent renewed, signals followed. A senior manager who governs her area through a stage-gated wheel, signing off at Business Acceptance and then relying on exception reports, will find it hard to show reasonable steps when something goes wrong in the space between sign-off and exception. A senior manager who governs through the plateaus will have a trail — sensemaking notes, line register, probe log, consent records with dissent, signal register — that shows her attention was continuous.

The SM&CR, in other words, puts accountability at knots. The rhizome supplies the lines between the knots, along which the accountable person learns what she needs to know.

9.4 Where the name goes

In Figure 3 the methodology's name has moved from the hub to a gold card at the lower left, linked to Enter in the middle, Compose, Map, Enunciate and Follow. This is not a demotion. It is a change of function.

At the hub, the name functioned as a centre of significance: the thing every stage was an instance of, the brand under which all change was done. In the corner, linked like the others, it functions as one more node: a description of the practice that the practice itself can revise. It has inputs — from sensing, from maps, from signals — and it has outputs — into composition and enunciation. The methodology is now something the practice uses and changes, not something it serves.

This matters because methodologies, once they become centres, tend to become ends in themselves. Change programmes become exercises in methodology compliance; the question did we follow the method? displaces the question did the change work? Power's rituals of verification apply to methodologies as much as to controls. A methodology that sits as one node among others is less likely to become a ritual, because it is constantly being fed by, and answering to, the other plateaus.

9.5 Containing anxiety without a hub

Chapter 2 suggested that the arborescent methodology functions partly as a social defence against anxiety, in the sense Isabel Menzies Lyth gave the term. Change in a regulated firm is frightening; the wheel contains the fright by promising order. Remove the hub and the promise, and where does the anxiety go?

The question is serious, and the psychoanalytic tradition on which Stacey and his colleagues drew gives a precise way to think about it. Wilfred Bion described the relation between a container and the contained: a mother who can take in her infant's unbearable feelings, hold them, and hand them back in a bearable form enables the infant to develop a capacity to think. When the container fails — when the feelings have nowhere to go — thinking becomes impossible, and the feelings are acted out instead. Organisations need containers in the same sense: arrangements that can take in the anxieties of the people working in them, hold them, and return them in a form that allows people to keep thinking.

The wheel contains by false promise: follow the stages and you will be safe. The promise works until it is tested, and then it fails catastrophically, because the anxiety it contained was never worked through, only deferred. The counter-design must contain differently, and it does so in three ways.

The refrain. Territorialise lightly is, among other things, a container. It gives people routines they can rely on — a stable place to stand while the ground moves. The refrain does not promise that nothing will change. It promises that there is a known way to work today, and a known way to raise it if today's way stops working.

The records. The consent record and the line register are containers in a literal sense: they hold, in writing, what has been agreed, what is rigid, what is negotiable, what was disputed. People who know that their dissent has been recorded can stop carrying it alone. People who know what is held rigid know what they need not worry about renegotiating.

The relationships. Above all, containment in an organisation is done by people for people: the manager who takes a worried employee's concern seriously and helps her think it through; the second-line specialist who can hear a front-line team's frustration with a control and work out with them what the control is for. The plateaus are designed to multiply such relationships — every plateau is a place where people meet across boundaries — rather than to route every anxiety upward to a hub that cannot possibly hold it all.

A hubless practice, then, is not a practice without holding. It is a practice in which holding is distributed, as accountability is distributed — at many knots, joined by many lines.

Scholium 8

Patricia Shaw and Ralph Stacey both insist that the stance of the practitioner in a complex organisation is participation, not control: she is in the conversation, not above it. Chris Mowles has described the demands this makes as decentring oneself, accepting radical contingency, being less determined to subdue the world and more willing to make meaning with others. That stance is itself a kind of containment. A leader who does not pretend to stand at the centre, and does not need to, can stay in the anxiety with others rather than passing it down the tree. It is harder than issuing a methodology. It is also more honest.

9.6 What the board sees

A last objection: boards and regulators need a single picture. They cannot read eight plateaus and four registers; they need to know, in summary, whether change is under control. Does the hubless practice deny them that?

It does not, but it changes what the summary is a summary of. Instead of a red-amber-green status for each stage of each change programme, the board receives readings of the registers: which molar lines were breached and corrected, which molecular lines were recalibrated and why, which consents were renewed or withdrawn, which signals were followed and what was found, which probes were amplified and which were stopped. That is a richer and more honest picture than a status dashboard, and it is one a board can actually use to exercise judgement. Chapter 12 sets it out in detail.

What the board loses is the comfort of a single centre that reports everything is green. What it gains is a view of the firm as it actually is — a centreless network with many knots of accountability, many lines of connection, and a standing capacity to notice what is escaping. Boards that have lived through a crisis will recognise which picture they would rather have had.

10The Three Lines: Molar, Molecular, Flight

Part III turns from concept to practice, and its foundation is a single distinction. Deleuze and Guattari argue that every individual, group and society is composed of three kinds of line. A governance practice that learns to tell them apart, and to treat each as it deserves, has the core of the counter-design. Figure 5 shows the distinction at work on the wheel's most revealing phrase.

10.1 The source: segmentarity

The ninth plateau of A Thousand Plateaus, titled Micropolitics and Segmentarity, begins from an anthropological observation. Human beings are segmented in every direction: by class, by age, by sex, by occupation, by territory, by the binary choices that sort us — man or woman, adult or child, worker or boss. Societies differ not in whether they are segmented but in how. So-called primitive societies, Deleuze and Guattari suggest, have supple segmentarity: segments that are mobile, overlapping, renegotiated in each encounter. Modern states have rigid segmentarity: segments that are fixed, binary, organised around a central point and overcoded by a single apparatus.

But — and this is the move that makes the analysis useful — modern societies do not lose supple segmentarity. They have both, at once, everywhere. Beneath every rigid segment there is a molecular flux, and the two kinds of line are entangled. And there is a third line, which is neither segment nor flux: the line of flight, which carries things out of the segmentation altogether.

The eighth plateau, 1874: Three Novellas, or "What Happened?", gives the same analysis at the scale of a life. Every life, Deleuze and Guattari suggest, reading stories by Henry James and F. Scott Fitzgerald, is composed of a rigid line of segments (school, then army, then career; marriage, then children), a supple line of molecular changes that do not register on the segments (a slight shift of mood, a crack that nobody notices), and a line of flight on which something breaks and one becomes someone else. Fitzgerald's essay The Crack-Up is their emblem: the great breaks are visible on the rigid line; the real crack happened earlier, silently, on the supple one.

10.2 Molar lines

Molar lines are rigid, segmented and binary. They divide the world into clear categories — permitted or prohibited, approved or not, inside the limit or outside it — and they are typically overcoded by a central apparatus that guarantees their uniformity. The word molar comes from chemistry by way of physics: a mole is a quantity of substance considered as a mass, statistically, without regard to the behaviour of its individual molecules.

In a regulated firm the molar lines are numerous and necessary. They include statutory and regulatory obligations; prescribed responsibilities and the allocation of accountability; risk limits and capital requirements; authorisation chains and segregation of duties; data-protection rules; conduct rules for individuals; contractual obligations to customers and counterparties. They are the lines along which the firm must behave uniformly whatever the local circumstances, because the law, the public interest or simple prudence requires it.

The counter-design's posture towards molar lines is hold rigid. This is where the wheel's disciplines apply in full: clear design, documented procedure, testing, sign-off, monitoring, correction of breaches. Nothing in the rhizome dissolves them. On the contrary, the counter-design argues that molar lines can be held more firmly once they are distinguished from everything else, because enforcement energy is no longer spread across every variance in the firm.

The danger on molar lines is the one Deleuze and Guattari name as the danger of rigid segmentarity: fear. An organisation that fears losing its segments clings to them, multiplies them, extends them to everything. Every practice becomes a control; every judgement becomes a rule; every variance becomes a breach. The result is not more safety but less, because a firm with a control for everything has attention for nothing.

10.3 Molecular lines

Molecular lines are supple. They are made of small, local, continuous changes that do not respect the boundaries of the molar segments: shifts of practice, of interpretation, of habit, of mood. They run beneath and across the rigid lines, and they are where most of the real life of an organisation happens. Hollnagel's work-as-done is molecular; so is Stacey's shadow system; so is Scott's mētis.

In a regulated firm molecular lines include the way a procedure is actually interpreted in a team; the informal division of labour that makes a process work; the judgement calls frontline staff make within their mandates; the scripts that drift as people find better words; the workarounds that compensate for a system's gaps; the local cultures of teams and branches; the relationships through which help is sought and given.

The counter-design's posture towards molecular lines is keep supple. They are not to be hardened into molar lines by default — that would destroy the adaptiveness that makes things go right. Nor are they to be ignored. They are governed by attention: noticed, mapped, discussed, and recalibrated when they drift across a molar line or when a practice proves so valuable it should be standardised.

The danger on molecular lines Deleuze and Guattari call clarity — the false clarity of supple segmentation, in which small groups reproduce, at their own scale, the rigidity of the whole. A team that has escaped the bureaucracy of head office can become its own little bureaucracy, with its own unwritten rules enforced more harshly than any procedure. Micro-fascisms, they call these at the extreme. Molecular does not mean benign.

10.4 Lines of flight

Lines of flight are neither rigid nor supple; they are escapes. They carry something out of every existing segmentation — not into another segment but into the unclassified. A new kind of customer need, a technology that makes an old control irrelevant, a pattern of complaints that fits no taxonomy, an employee who leaves and says something nobody wanted to hear, a quiet change in how a product is being used: these are lines of flight.

The counter-design's posture is follow, don't suppress. Lines of flight are where the future arrives first, usually disguised as error. A practice that files them as exceptions loses its earliest warnings and its best opportunities at once.

The danger on lines of flight is the gravest of the three, and Deleuze and Guattari are blunt about it. A line of flight can turn into a line of abolition — destruction pure and simple. The rogue trader's line of flight carried a bank to collapse. A mis-selling culture often begins as one team's line of flight: a clever reinterpretation of a target that escapes the categories of the incentive scheme. Following a line of flight is therefore always a matter of judgement: understand where it is going, then amplify, dampen or stop it. What is ruled out is not stopping a line of flight — some must be stopped at once — but stopping it without having looked.

THE FUTURE ARRIVES FIRST AS ERROR. THE QUESTION IS WHETHER ANYONE IS LOOKING.

10.5 Three lines, not three lines of defence

Governance professionals will hear an echo of a more familiar triad, the Three Lines Model of the Institute of Internal Auditors (formerly the three lines of defence): management as the first line, owning risk; risk and compliance functions as the second, overseeing and challenging; internal audit as the third, providing independent assurance. The echo is not intended as a pun, but it is instructive.

The Three Lines Model divides roles: who does what with respect to risk. The three lines of this chapter divide kinds of thing: what sort of line a given element of the firm is. The two triads are orthogonal. Each of the IIA's three lines must deal with all three of Deleuze's. Management must hold molar lines, keep molecular lines supple and notice lines of flight in its own operations. The second line must challenge where molar lines are drawn, watch molecular lines for drift and help follow lines of flight. Internal audit must test molar lines hard, sample molecular lines for drift across molar boundaries, and — the role audit is least used to — read the signal register to see whether the firm is noticing what is escaping.

The value of setting the two triads against each other is that it reveals a gap in the familiar model. The Three Lines Model says a great deal about who holds molar lines and almost nothing about molecular lines or lines of flight. A firm that implements it faithfully can still have nobody whose job is to notice the supple drift of practice or the first signs of the new. The counter-design fills that gap by making line-reading a capacity of all three roles.

10.6 Calibration: drawing the lines in the right places

The crucial practical question is not what the three lines are but where each element of a given firm belongs. That is a matter of calibration, and it is never settled once and for all.

Some elements are molar by necessity: the law makes them so. A capital requirement, a client-money rule, a prohibition on certain product features, a prescribed responsibility. There is no calibration question about whether to hold these rigidly; the only question is how.

Many elements are molar by choice: the firm has decided to make them rigid. A policy that every complaint over a certain value goes to a senior handler; a rule that every new supplier is assessed by a central team; a standard script for a sensitive conversation. These choices should be revisited. Some will turn out to protect something important. Others will turn out to be rigid only because nobody has dared to relax them, and their rigidity will be costing the firm adaptiveness for no gain.

Many elements are molecular by default: nobody has decided anything about them, and they flex as practice flexes. Some of these should be made molar, because their variation is now affecting outcomes the firm is accountable for. The Consumer Duty, with its focus on outcomes, has forced many firms to discover molecular practices — a handler's discretion, a script's variations — that were driving customer outcomes without anyone governing them.

Calibration is the practice of moving elements between lines deliberately, with reasons, recorded in the line register and approved by the accountable person. It is the counter-design's answer to the wheel's materiality assessment: not is this exception material? but is this element on the right line?

11Six Heuristics in Place of Four Virtues

The 2015 wheel names four virtues at its centre: disciplined, collaborative, adaptive, effective. They are good virtues, and nobody would argue against them. But they tell the practitioner what to be, not what to do, and they are silent about the situations in which they conflict. The counter-design offers instead six heuristics — rules of thumb for practice in the complex domain. Each is short enough to remember in a meeting. Each is developed here with its sources, an example, and its limit.

11.1 Why heuristics, not principles

Gerd Gigerenzer and his colleagues have shown over three decades that in uncertain environments simple heuristics often outperform complex optimising models, because they are robust to the noise that over-fitted models chase. A heuristic is not a degraded rule; it is a tool matched to a world in which the information needed for optimisation is not available. Complexity science predicts the same: when a system is incompressible, the best guidance is not a complete model but a small number of simple rules that interact well with the system's own dynamics.

Snowden makes a related point about what he calls enabling constraints: in the complex domain, behaviour is shaped less by rigid governing rules than by a few simple boundaries and attractors that allow patterns to form. The six heuristics are intended as enabling constraints of this kind. They do not specify what to do in any given case. They shape the space in which the practitioner decides.

11.2 A · Enter anywhere

The heuristic. Any node can start the work. Let the trigger choose the door.

Its ground. The rhizome has no beginning; it is always in the middle. In practice, change in a regulated firm is triggered by events — a complaint pattern, a regulatory publication, an audit finding, a technology failure, a staff suggestion, a board question — and each kind of trigger points naturally to a different plateau. A methodology that forces every trigger through the same first stage wastes time and, worse, loses the specific knowledge that the trigger carried.

An example. A supervisor's letter warns that firms in the sector are failing to identify customers in financial difficulty early enough. The wheel would begin with discovery of the current state. The heuristic suggests starting at Read the lines: which of our obligations here are molar (the rules on forbearance), which practices are molecular (how collectors spot difficulty), and where might something already be escaping (customers in difficulty who never contact us)? That reading points straight to a probe and a map, and discovery happens through them.

Its limit. Entering anywhere is not entering nowhere. Every entry must quickly connect to the other plateaus. A change that enters at Experiment and never visits Read the lines may probe across a molar line without noticing.

11.3 B · Connect anything to anything

The heuristic. Regulation to culture, data to rumour, a control to the person who routes round it.

Its ground. The first and second principles of the rhizome: connection and heterogeneity. Most of the failures documented in post-crisis inquiries were failures of connection — information held in one part of the firm that would have changed decisions in another, if only it had travelled. Arborescent structures connect through common ancestors; the heuristic insists on direct lateral connection.

An example. An operational risk team notices that a particular control is frequently overridden. The arborescent response is to report the override rate up the tree. The heuristic asks who does the overriding, and why, and connects the control owner directly to them. Often the override turns out to be compensating for a gap that nobody upstream knew existed — and the people overriding are the firm's best source of knowledge about what the control should be.

Its limit. Some connections must not be made. Information barriers, conflicts of interest and data-protection rules are molar lines precisely because certain connections are harmful. Connect anything to anything operates within those walls, not through them.

11.4 C · Write at n − 1

The heuristic. Subtract the master plan and keep the multiplicity. No single owner of the whole.

Its ground. The third principle, multiplicity, and Deleuze and Guattari's instruction to subtract the unifying One. In practice the One appears as the master plan, the target operating model, the single source of truth, the enterprise-wide framework. These are not useless; they are useful tracings. The danger is mistaking them for the territory, and assuming that the firm's many practices are deviations from the plan rather than the plan being one simplification among many.

An example. A firm launches a culture programme with a single statement of values and a set of desired behaviours. Writing at n − 1 means treating the statement as one node among the firm's many local cultures — listening to how each team actually interprets the values, mapping where interpretations diverge, and letting the statement be revised by what the listening finds. The programme becomes a conversation among cultures rather than a cascade from one.

Its limit. Accountability is not subtracted. Somebody owns the outcome under SM&CR. What is subtracted is the pretence that owning the outcome means owning a single plan that determines it.

11.5 D · Expect rupture

The heuristic. When a line breaks, do not mourn it; look where it regrows. Fragility is information.

Its ground. The fourth principle, asignifying rupture, and the broader complexity insight that complex systems fail as a matter of course. Charles Perrow's Normal Accidents (1984) argued that in tightly coupled, interactively complex systems, accidents are not aberrations but normal — the inevitable product of the system's structure. Operational resilience regulation takes the same view. The heuristic converts the inevitability into a practice: every break is examined for what it reveals about where the system's real lines run.

An example. A core system outage forces a branch network onto manual procedures for a day. Most firms run a post-incident review focused on the cause of the outage. The heuristic adds a second review, focused on the recovery: which manual procedures worked, who knew them, which informal networks were activated, what customers experienced. That second review maps the rhizome the outage exposed — and it is the map the firm will need next time.

Its limit. Expecting rupture does not mean courting it. Safe-to-fail probes are bounded; molar lines are held. The heuristic is about how to learn from breaks, not a licence to cause them.

THE OUTAGE IS A PHOTOGRAPH OF THE RHIZOME. DEVELOP IT BEFORE IT FADES.

11.6 E · Work with shì 勢

The heuristic. Read the propensity of the situation and lean into it, rather than forcing a designed outcome.

Its ground. François Jullien's studies of Chinese strategic thought, above all The Propensity of Things (1992) and A Treatise on Efficacy (1996), contrast a Western model of action — set a goal, make a plan, impose it on the world — with a Chinese model in which the skilled actor reads the configuration of forces in a situation (shì) and intervenes at the point where a small push will let the situation's own propensity carry the result. Water, in Sunzi's image, does not force its way; it follows the terrain and, gathered, moves boulders. This book's author has long used the triad dào, lǐ and shì — way, pattern and propensity — as a spine for thinking about organisations; here shì takes the lead. It is the Chinese name for what complexity science calls attractors and what Deleuze calls tendencies.

An example. A firm wants frontline staff to escalate concerns earlier. The forcing approach mandates escalation and measures compliance. The shì approach first reads where escalation is already happening — which teams do it, why, through whom — and then removes the small obstacles that stop it happening elsewhere, so that the propensity already present can spread. The second approach is slower to announce and faster to work.

Its limit. Some situations tend the wrong way. A culture tending towards mis-selling must be redirected, not ridden. Reading propensity is the first step; deciding whether to lean into it or against it is a judgement the heuristic does not make for you.

11.7 F · Hold the tree where it is owed

The heuristic. Law, accountability and capital keep their trunk. Everything else earns its rigidity.

Its ground. Deleuze and Guattari's refusal of the simple opposition between tree and rhizome, and the analysis of molar lines in Chapter 10. This heuristic is the counterweight to the other five. Without it, the counter-design could be read as a licence to loosen everything. With it, the rhizome is situated within the obligations a regulated firm cannot negotiate.

An example. A team proposes, in the spirit of experimentation, to let call handlers waive certain fees at their discretion for customers in difficulty. The heuristic asks first which molar lines the proposal touches — fair treatment rules, the firm's fair-value assessment, the rules on vulnerable customers, financial controls on revenue — and whether the discretion can be bounded so that those lines hold. If it can, the probe runs. If not, the tree is owed, and the probe is redesigned.

Its limit. Owed is the operative word. Rigidity must be justified by something the law, accountability or prudence actually requires. A rule kept rigid only because it has always been rigid has not earned its trunk.

11.8 Using the heuristics together

The six heuristics are designed to be in tension. A and B push outwards, towards more entries and more connections. C and E push against control from a single point. D pushes towards learning from failure. F pulls back towards the rigid lines that must hold. Practice in the complex domain is the art of holding these tensions without collapsing into any one of them: neither the rigid firm that controls everything nor the loose firm that controls nothing, but the firm that knows which of its lines are which and treats each accordingly.

A team can use them as a checklist at the start of any piece of work. Where are we entering, and does that door suit the trigger? What could we connect that we have not? Where are we pretending there is one plan? What would we learn if this broke? Which way is the situation already tending? And which of our lines are genuinely owed to the tree? Six questions, a few minutes, and the work is already oriented differently from a wheel.

12The Evidence Trail: What the Board, the Auditor and the Supervisor Still See

A rhizomatic practice that could not be evidenced would be useless in a regulated firm, however true to the world it was. Boards must oversee; auditors must assure; supervisors must judge; accountable executives must show reasonable steps. This chapter sets out the evidence trail the counter-design produces — six standing instruments, each descended from an item of the wheel's continuous-improvement bar — and argues that it gives those readers a better view than the wheel's status reports ever did.

12.1 The audit society and its discontents

Michael Power's The Audit Society (1997) and The Risk Management of Everything (2004) describe an institutional world in which verification has become an end in itself. Organisations are made auditable — their activities recorded, standardised and evidenced in forms an auditor can check — and the making-auditable reshapes the activities. What can be evidenced comes to count; what cannot be evidenced comes to seem unreal. Power called the resulting practices rituals of verification: they produce comfort, and they produce defensibility, but they do not necessarily produce control.

The wheel is well adapted to the audit society. Each stage has a deliverable; each deliverable can be checked; the sign-off at Business Acceptance is a perfect auditable moment; exception counts are easy to report. Its evidence trail answers the question did we follow the method? very well. It answers the question is the change working, and would we know if it stopped? much less well.

The counter-design does not escape the audit society, and does not try to. It proposes a different set of things to evidence: not stages completed but lines held, consents sustained, probes run and signals followed. These are still auditable. They are simply closer to the things that actually determine outcomes.

12.2 Six instruments

Each of the six items of the old continuous-improvement bar becomes a standing instrument, opened on the first day of a change rather than after embedding. The specimen fields below are deliberately minimal; a firm will adapt them to its own systems and taxonomy.

I. The probe log (from Test)

A record of every safe-to-fail experiment. Fields: the probe's name and owner; the composition it tests; the hypothesis; the molar lines it touches and how each is protected; the stated signals of success and of harm; the customer-impact review before start; dates; what happened; the decision — amplify, dampen, stop — with reasons; what was learned. The probe log answers the supervisor's natural worry about experimentation (were customers put at risk?) with a positive record: every probe was bounded, reviewed and decided.

II. The line register (from Assure)

For each important service or process, a register of its lines. Fields: the element (a rule, a practice, a control, a script, a discretion); its line — molar, molecular, or flagged as a line of flight; for molar entries, the source of rigidity (statute, regulation, contract, accountability, board policy) and the control that holds it; for molecular entries, the range of variation observed and the conditions that would make it molar; the person who decided the classification; the date of last review. Assurance tests molar entries hard and samples molecular entries for drift across molar boundaries. The register makes the firm's calibration explicit, and therefore challengeable.

III. The consent record (from Feedback)

The written form of each enunciation. Fields: what is agreed; who took part, by role; the accountable executive; conditions; duration and review triggers; what is held rigid and what is left to judgement; dissent, with names (where people are willing) and reasons; dates of renewal and what changed at each. For a senior manager under SM&CR, the consent record is direct evidence of reasonable steps: consent was sought from the right people, challenge was heard, conditions were set, and the agreement was revisited when circumstances changed.

IV. The correction log (from Remediate)

The record of molar breaches and their remediation. Fields: the breach; the molar line breached; customers or counterparties affected; immediate correction; notification where required; root cause; changes made; verification. This looks much like a traditional issues log, and deliberately so: molar breaches deserve the full rigour of established remediation practice. The difference is scope. Because only molar breaches enter here — molecular drift and lines of flight are logged elsewhere — the correction log is not swamped by harmless variation, and its contents can be acted on with the seriousness they deserve.

V. Line calibration (from Assess materiality)

The record of decisions to move an element between lines. Fields: the element; its old and new line; the reason (for example, a molecular practice now shown to affect customer outcomes, or a molar rule shown to protect nothing); the evidence; the decision-maker; the date. Calibration replaces the wheel's materiality assessment with a question that is more useful in the complex domain: not is this deviation large enough to matter? but is this element on the right line?

VI. The signal register (from Evolve techniques)

The record of lines of flight. Fields: what was noticed, by whom, when; where it was followed and what was found; the triage decision — correct, discuss, probe, monitor, stop; the owner; follow-up. The bar to entry is deliberately low. Read periodically as a whole, the register is the firm's peripheral vision: the place where emerging risks and opportunities first appear, before they have names.

12.3 What the board sees

Boards are usually given change and risk information as status: red, amber, green, by programme, by stage, by risk category. The status form has one virtue — it is quick to read — and many vices. It aggregates away context, it rewards the reporting of green, and it describes the method's progress rather than the world's response.

The counter-design gives the board readings of the six instruments instead. A quarterly reading might say: these molar lines were breached and corrected, with these root causes; these elements were recalibrated between lines, for these reasons; these consents were renewed, amended or withdrawn; these probes were amplified, dampened or stopped; these signals were followed, and this is what they suggest about where the firm's risks are moving. Each reading takes a little longer to absorb than a dashboard. Each gives the board something to exercise judgement on, rather than something to note.

Above all, the signal register gives a board what status reports cannot: a view of what does not yet fit. Every major governance failure of the past thirty years was preceded by signals that existed somewhere in the organisation and did not reach the board in a usable form. A board that reads the signal register is at least looking in the right direction.

12.4 What the auditor sees

Internal and external auditors can test each instrument. They can sample molar entries in the line register and test the controls that hold them, as they do now. They can sample molecular entries and check whether drift has crossed molar boundaries without recalibration. They can review the probe log for bounding and customer protection. They can examine consent records for completeness and renewal. They can test whether correction-log items were remediated and verified. And they can review the signal register for whether signals were followed and decided, rather than merely logged.

This last test is new, and it is where audit can add most value to a complex-domain practice. It asks not was the procedure followed? but is the organisation noticing what is happening to it? That is a question auditors are well placed to ask, because they stand outside the line management that has the strongest incentives not to notice.

12.5 What the supervisor sees

Supervisors of financial firms have moved, over the past decade, towards judgement-based supervision focused on outcomes, culture, governance and resilience. The six instruments speak to each of these. Outcomes: the line register shows which practices drive outcomes and how they are governed; the correction log shows where outcomes went wrong and what was done. Culture: the consent records and signal register show whether challenge is invited and heard, and whether anomalies are surfaced or suppressed. Governance: the instruments together show where accountability sits and how it is discharged. Resilience: the maps' recovery lines and the probe log show how the firm learns and adapts.

A supervisor reading these instruments sees a firm that knows which of its lines are rigid, which are supple and which are escaping, and can show what it has done about each. That is a more convincing picture than a set of completed methodology stages, and it is closer to what supervisors say they want.

EVIDENCE WHAT DETERMINES OUTCOMES, NOT WHAT DETERMINES COMPLIANCE WITH THE METHOD.

12.6 Safety-II and the evidence of things going right

Hollnagel distinguishes Safety-I, which understands safety as the absence of failures and studies what goes wrong, from Safety-II, which understands safety as the presence of the capacity to succeed under varying conditions and studies what goes right. Most governance evidence is Safety-I: incidents, breaches, exceptions, findings. It tells a board how often things failed. It says almost nothing about why they usually do not.

The counter-design's instruments include Safety-II evidence. The maps record how work flexes to succeed. The line register records the molecular practices that make things go right. The probe log records what the system amplified. The signal register records, alongside threats, the emerging practices that might be worth spreading. A firm that collects this evidence knows something most firms do not: where its resilience actually comes from. That is the knowledge it will need when the next severe-but-plausible scenario stops being a scenario.

12.7 Proportionality

A reasonable objection: six instruments sound like a great deal of paperwork. The counter-design does not ask that every small change carry a full set. It asks that the instruments be proportionate to the stakes and the complexity. A minor, clearly ordered change — a new form field, a routine system patch — may need no more than a line in the line register and a short consent record. A major change in a complex area — a new product for vulnerable customers, a migration of a core system, a response to a new regulatory regime — warrants the full set. The heuristic is simple: the more the change touches molecular lines and lines of flight, and the higher the stakes, the more of the trail it should leave.

In most firms, moreover, the instruments are not additions but replacements. The probe log replaces test plans and pilot reports; the line register replaces parts of the control library and process documentation; the consent record replaces sign-off packs; the correction log is the existing issues log, narrowed; line calibration replaces materiality assessments; and the signal register replaces exception reports. The paperwork is redistributed, not multiplied, and redirected towards what matters.

12.8 A specimen board reading

To make the argument concrete, here is the shape of a quarterly reading a board might receive for a single important business service — a retail lender's arrears and collections service, say — in place of a status dashboard. It is illustrative, and deliberately brief.

Molar lines. Two breaches recorded in the correction log this quarter. One: a forbearance letter sent with an incorrect statutory notice, affecting forty customers, corrected within five working days, root cause a template change not routed through legal review; the routing has been fixed and verified. Two: a segregation-of-duties gap in a payment-arrangement override, identified by the second line, closed, no customer detriment found. No other molar entries breached; testing of the remaining molar entries in the line register found them holding.

Calibration. One element moved from molecular to molar: collectors' discretion over the length of payment breaks, after the line register showed that variation in its use was producing materially different outcomes for similar customers. One element moved from molar to molecular: a rule requiring a manager's approval for every income-and-expenditure reassessment, after evidence that the approval step added delay without catching errors. Both decisions were taken by the accountable executive with the reasons recorded.

Consent. The collections operating consent was renewed after its complaint trigger fired. One dissent, from a team leader concerned about workload under the new pre-emptive contact routine, is recorded and has been followed by a staffing review.

Probes. Three probes ran. Pre-emptive contact using rate-expiry data was amplified to two further teams. A redesigned options letter was amplified and is being embedded. A self-service payment-plan tool was dampened after it showed lower take-up among customers flagged as vulnerable; it remains available but is no longer the default route.

Signals. Eleven entries in the signal register. Three were triaged as molar and passed to correction; five as molecular drift and discussed with teams; three remain open as lines of flight, the most significant being a pattern of self-employed borrowers whose income evidence does not fit the affordability model. A probe has been designed and is awaiting customer-impact review.

A board member reading this has something to ask about on every line: why the template change bypassed legal review, whether the workload dissent is resolved, what the self-employed pattern implies for lending policy upstream. That is the purpose of the reading. A dashboard invites a board to note; a reading invites it to govern. The difference, over years, is the difference between a board that is told things are under control and one that knows where they are not.

13How the Rhizome Fails

A treatise that proposed a counter-design without saying how it goes wrong would be repeating the wheel's error in a new key: offering a method as if it were safe. Deleuze and Guattari are unusually candid about the dangers of their own concepts, and complexity science has its own catalogue of failure. This chapter gathers both into six failure modes of the rhizomatic practice, with the guardrails that the counter-design builds against each.

13.1 Recapture: the rhizome that becomes a tree again

Deleuze and Guattari warn that rhizomes constantly re-form trees: points of structuration appear, hierarchies harden, a line that was open becomes a path everyone must take. This is not a malfunction; it is what assemblages do. Territorialisation is a tendency, and it is strongest where anxiety is high.

In practice, recapture looks like this. The plateaus are introduced; within a year, a governance office has published a plateau procedure, each plateau has a template, and change programmes are required to evidence completion of all eight in order. The rhizome has become a wheel with eight segments instead of seven. The consent record has become a sign-off form. The signal register has become an exception log under a new name.

Guardrail. The counter-design builds in its own instability. Version n − 1 means the practice describes itself as never finished. The name-node is linked to the signal register, so that signs of recapture — templates hardening, plateaus being sequenced, registers being filled in for form's sake — are logged as lines of flight in the practice itself. And the test for recapture is simple: has anyone entered at a plateau other than the first this quarter? If not, the rhizome has become a tree.

13.2 Consultancy capture: the rhizome as a product

A related failure is commercial. Every successful idea in management is eventually packaged, branded, certified and sold. Agile, which began as a short manifesto of values, became within two decades a certification industry with frameworks of extraordinary arborescent complexity. The rhizome is not immune; indeed, its vocabulary is attractive precisely because it sounds sophisticated.

Guardrail. The counter-design is offered as a set of concepts and instruments, not as a proprietary method. Its claim is not that firms should buy it but that firms already do much of it, in the shadow system, and would do it better if they did it deliberately. A firm that pays for a rhizome transformation programme with a fixed scope, a target operating model and a go-live date has missed the point in the act of purchase.

A RHIZOME WITH A GO-LIVE DATE IS A TREE IN DISGUISE.

13.3 Micro-fascism: tyranny of the supple

Deleuze and Guattari's most uncomfortable warning is that supple, molecular segmentation can be more oppressive than rigid segmentation. When the formal hierarchy loosens, small groups often reproduce its rigidity at their own scale, with rules that are unwritten, enforced through belonging and exclusion, and impossible to appeal. They call these micro-fascisms, and they insist that every molecular movement carries the danger.

Organisations know the phenomenon well. The team that has escaped head-office bureaucracy and built its own culture, which turns out to be cliquish, unforgiving of dissent and hostile to outsiders. The flat structure in which nobody has formal authority and everybody knows who really decides. The informal coalition that controls what gets said in meetings. Stacey's own work on informal coalitions in organisations documented how shadow-system power works, and how it can be as coercive as any formal hierarchy, with the added disadvantage that it cannot be challenged through formal means because it does not officially exist.

Guardrail. The molar lines include the lines that protect individuals from informal power: whistleblowing arrangements, grievance procedures, conduct rules, the right to escalate. These are held rigid precisely because the molecular lines can turn oppressive. And the consent record's insistence on recorded dissent is a direct defence: in a micro-fascist group, dissent is the first casualty; a practice that requires dissent to be written down makes its absence visible.

13.4 The black hole: lines of flight that destroy

The third danger is the one already met in Chapter 10: the line of flight that turns into a line of abolition. Deleuze and Guattari describe how a line of flight can lose its creative connection and become pure destruction — a passion for abolition, a black hole that swallows everything near it. They are thinking of political movements and individual lives. Governance professionals will think of rogue traders, frauds, cultures of mis-selling, and the charismatic leader who escapes every control because his results are so good.

A rhizomatic practice is, if anything, more exposed to this danger than an arborescent one, because it values lines of flight and is reluctant to suppress them. A firm that has learned to say follow, don't suppress may follow a fraud for too long, mistaking it for innovation.

Guardrail. Following is never endorsing, and the triage of Figure 5 has three exits, not one. Any line of flight that approaches a molar line is stopped at once, whatever its promise. The signal register records the triage decision and the owner, so that a decision to follow rather than stop is attributable and reviewable. And the molar lines around financial controls, segregation of duties and conduct are held especially rigid precisely because the black hole is real.

13.5 Romanticism: the cult of emergence

A quieter failure is intellectual. Complexity science and Deleuzian philosophy can be read as celebrations of emergence, novelty and flow, and some of their popularisers have read them that way. The resulting romanticism treats every anomaly as a precious sign of the new, every plan as an imposition, every control as a defence against life. It is attractive, and it is disastrous in a regulated firm.

The literature on complexity in organisations has its own diagnosis of this. Reviewers of the field have distinguished careful uses of complexity concepts from what one might call metaphorical licence — using words like emergence and edge of chaos as permission for whatever the speaker already wanted, without the discipline the concepts require. Stacey himself became increasingly critical of the way complexity ideas were used to promise managers new forms of control over emergence, which he regarded as a contradiction in terms.

Guardrail. The counter-design is not a celebration of emergence. It is a calibration: some lines rigid, some supple, some followed. Heuristic F — hold the tree where it is owed — is the explicit counterweight to romanticism. And the evidence trail requires that every probe be bounded and every signal be decided, which leaves no room for treating emergence as self-justifying.

13.6 Exhaustion: the cost of attention

The last failure is human. A practice built on continuous sensing, line-reading, mapping, probing, consent renewal and signal-following asks a great deal of attention from a great many people. Attention is finite. A firm that attempts all of it, everywhere, at full intensity, will exhaust its people and then fall back on the wheel out of sheer fatigue.

The wheel has one real advantage here: it economises on attention. Once a process is embedded, nobody need think about it until an exception appears. That is precisely its danger — it stops looking — but it is also why it is restful.

Guardrail. Proportionality (Chapter 12) is the main defence: the plateaus run at low intensity almost everywhere and at high intensity only where stakes and complexity warrant. The refrain is the second: Territorialise lightly exists so that most work, most of the time, can be done without reinventing it. And the third is distribution: because every team carries some capacity on every plateau, no single function is asked to sense the whole firm. The rhizome is not more work everywhere. It is attention redistributed to where it is needed, and released from where it is not.

13.7 The wheel's revenge

There is one more failure mode, and it is the most likely of all. A firm adopts the counter-design, runs it well for a year, and then a crisis hits — a regulatory investigation, a major incident, a change of chief executive. Under pressure, the firm reverts. The wheel returns, because the wheel promises control and the crisis has made control seem urgent. The registers are abandoned, the plateaus forgotten, and the firm goes back to designing the right solution and signing it off.

This is not a failure that any design can prevent. It is a feature of how organisations respond to anxiety: in crisis, they regress to the most familiar defence. The only guardrail is the one Bion's container suggests. If the counter-design has been containing anxiety well — through its refrains, its records and its relationships — then the crisis will have somewhere to go other than back to the wheel. If it has not, the wheel will return, and it will be welcomed.

That is why Chapter 9's argument about containment is not a side-issue. A rhizomatic practice that cannot hold people's fear will not survive its first serious test. One that can, will find that the crisis is just another plateau — entered in the middle, read for its lines, mapped, probed, consented to, territorialised lightly, and followed where it leads.

14Three Transpositions

Concepts earn their keep in cases. This chapter works through three illustrative transpositions — composite situations, built from the kinds of problem governance professionals meet, not accounts of any real firm. Each is run twice: once through the wheel, once through the assemblage. The point is not that the wheel fails in every respect, but to show, concretely, what the assemblage sees that the wheel does not.

14.1 Transposition one: arrears and the Consumer Duty

The situation. A mid-sized lender's arrears volumes are rising as fixed-rate deals expire into higher rates. The board, mindful of the Consumer Duty's demand for good outcomes and its attention to customers in vulnerable circumstances, asks for the collections and recoveries process to be reviewed and improved.

Through the wheel. A project is mobilised. Discover: analysts interview the collections team, review policies and gather metrics — arrears rates, cure rates, call volumes, complaint numbers. A current-state document is produced. Define and design: requirements are set — compliant forbearance, consistent treatment, identification of vulnerability — and a new target process is designed with improved scripts, a vulnerability flag and revised forbearance options. Review: compliance and legal challenge the design and some changes are made. Process mapping: the new process is mapped in swim lanes and walked through. Prototype and test: one team pilots the new process for six weeks; feedback is gathered and the scripts refined. Business acceptance: the credit risk committee approves and the accountable executive signs. Embedding: training, roll-out, monitoring. Exceptions are reported monthly.

The result is likely to be a real improvement. It is also likely to miss things. The current-state document will describe how the policy says collections works, adjusted by interviews in which collectors tell analysts what they think analysts want to hear. The single pilot will succeed, because pilots usually do. And the monthly exception report will count deviations from the new script without asking why collectors deviate.

Through the assemblage. The work enters in the middle, at the plateau where the trigger points: Follow the lines of flight. The signal register already contains entries from collectors and complaints handlers — customers saying they did not understand their options, a cluster of complaints from self-employed borrowers, a collector who has started calling certain customers before they miss a payment because she can see what is coming. These are followed. Read the lines: the forbearance rules and the treatment of vulnerability are molar; the scripts, the timing of calls and the collectors' judgement are molecular; the pre-emptive calls and the self-employed cluster are lines of flight. Map, don't trace: collectors draw on a wall how a difficult call actually goes, including the places where they leave the script and why; the official process diagram is laid over the drawing, and the gap between them becomes the agenda. Compose the assemblage: three compositions are sketched — one that formalises pre-emptive contact using rate-expiry data, one that redesigns the options letter with customers' help, one that gives experienced collectors more discretion within defined bounds. Experiment in parallel: all three run for six weeks in different teams, each bounded by a manual review of any adverse decision and by close monitoring of complaints and outcomes. Two take; one does not. Enunciate together: collectors, the vulnerability specialist, complaints, credit risk and the accountable executive agree a composition built from the two that took, with conditions, a review trigger tied to the arrears trajectory, and a dissent from one team leader about workload recorded. Territorialise lightly: the new routine is trained and embedded, with an exit named — if pre-emptive contact begins to feel intrusive to customers, as measured by complaints, it will be paused.

What the assemblage saw. A collector's line of flight — calling customers before they miss a payment — that the wheel would have recorded as an exception to the script, and that turned out to be the most effective intervention in the programme. A group of customers, the self-employed, whose difficulty the policy had not anticipated. A workload risk recorded as dissent before it became a conduct problem. And an evidence trail that shows the board, and the supervisor if asked, not merely that a process was redesigned but that outcomes were sensed, experiments bounded, consent sought and the firm's attention kept open.

14.2 Transposition two: operational resilience mapping

The situation. A firm must identify its important business services, set impact tolerances, map the resources each service depends on, and test its ability to stay within tolerance in severe but plausible scenarios. The regulatory requirement is detailed; the deadline is fixed.

Through the wheel. The firm runs a resilience programme. Services are identified, tolerances set by committee, and each service mapped to its people, processes, technology, facilities and information. The maps are validated with process owners and signed off. Scenario tests are run as desk exercises against the maps. Vulnerabilities are logged as exceptions and remediated.

The weakness is subtle. The maps describe how each service runs when it runs normally. They are tracings. But resilience is not about normal running; it is about what happens when something breaks. And when something breaks, recovery happens along lines the normal maps do not show — manual fallbacks known to a few people, informal networks across teams, a supplier contact someone happens to have, a spreadsheet kept in a drawer.

Through the assemblage. The molar lines are clear and held rigid: the regulatory requirements, the tolerances once set, the accountabilities. Within them the work is done on the plateaus. Map, don't trace is the centre of gravity: each service is mapped twice, once as it normally runs (the tracing) and once as it recovers (the map), and the recovery map is drawn with the people who have actually recovered it before, including the informal lines. Expect rupture becomes a literal method: past incidents are mined for what they revealed about the rhizome. Experiment in parallel: rather than a single annual desk exercise, a portfolio of small live tests — switching a team to manual for an hour, withdrawing a key person from a process for a day — is run under tight bounds, each recorded in the probe log. Follow the lines of flight: the tests surface surprises, which go to the signal register, including the discovery that two supposedly independent services depend on the same individual's knowledge. Enunciate together: the tolerances, initially set by committee, are revisited with the people who run the services and the consent records note where operators believe tolerances are unrealistic.

What the assemblage saw. The difference between how services run and how they recover — which is the whole of operational resilience — and a dependency on single individuals that no tracing would reveal. It also produced a more honest self-assessment, because the consent records captured operators' doubts about tolerances that the committee had set without them.

Scholium 9

Operational resilience regulation is perhaps the most explicitly complexity-aware regulatory regime yet written. It assumes failure will happen; it asks for tolerance rather than prevention; it focuses on services as experienced by customers rather than systems as owned by departments; it demands testing against severe but plausible scenarios rather than probable ones. Read through this book's lens, it is a regulator asking firms to map their rhizomes. The irony is that many firms have responded by running it through a wheel.

14.3 Transposition three: a receivership

The situation. A lender appoints a fixed-charge receiver over a mixed-use commercial property after the borrower defaults. The property has retail units on the ground floor, some vacant, and residential flats above, several let on assured shorthold tenancies, one occupied by a person whose status is unclear. The building has maintenance problems, the borrower is uncooperative, and the lender wants to maximise recovery within a reasonable time without reputational damage.

Through the wheel. Recovery processes in lenders are often designed like the wheel: instruct a valuation, secure the asset, collect rents, prepare for sale, market, accept the best offer, complete, report. Each step has a template and a sign-off. Deviations are exceptions.

Any experienced receiver knows this is not how a receivership goes. The valuation depends on the lettings strategy, which depends on the repairs, which depend on the budget, which depends on the lender's appetite, which depends on the valuation. The unclear occupant may be a tenant with protected rights, a licensee, a trespasser or a vulnerable person, and the answer changes the legal position, the timetable and the reputational risk. The uncooperative borrower may have information the receiver needs, and may also have claims. Every step is connected to every other.

Through the assemblage. The receivership is read as an assemblage from the start: the building and its physical condition; the tenants and their legal positions; the occupant; the borrower; the security documentation and its powers; the lender's provisioning and appetite; the local market and its comparables; the receiver's statutory and professional duties to the lender and to the borrower; the timetable. Read the lines: the receiver's powers under the charge and statute, the tenants' statutory protections, the duties of care owed on sale, health and safety obligations to occupants, and anti-money-laundering requirements are molar and held rigid. The lettings strategy, the repairs programme, the marketing approach and the negotiation with the borrower are molecular. The unclear occupant is a line of flight — something that fits no category yet — and is followed carefully before anything is decided, because the cost of misclassifying a vulnerable person is high in every sense. Compose the assemblage: several recovery compositions are sketched — sell as is, repair and re-let the retail then sell, sell the flats individually, negotiate a consensual sale with the borrower — each with its expected capacities and risks. Experiment in parallel, at the small scale a receivership allows: a soft marketing test to gauge investor appetite, a conversation with the borrower to see whether cooperation is possible, a quote for the most urgent repairs. Enunciate together: the receiver and the lender agree a strategy with conditions and triggers, and record the receiver's reservations where the lender's timetable conflicts with good practice. The strategy is territorialised lightly: committed to, but with named exits if the market moves or the occupant's position changes.

What the assemblage saw. That the steps are not a sequence but a web, which experienced practitioners already know and which the process template denies. That the unclear occupant is the most important line in the case — legally, ethically and reputationally — and must be followed before it is classified. And that the receiver's professional judgement, documented through the consent record and the line register, is better evidence of proper conduct than a completed template.

EVERY WORKOUT IS AN ASSEMBLAGE. THE TEMPLATE IS ONLY ITS TRACING.

14.4 What the three cases share

Three features recur. In each case, the wheel's front-loaded discovery would have missed something that the assemblage's continuous sensing found: a collector's improvised practice, a hidden single-person dependency, an occupant who fits no category. In each case, the decisive intervention came from a line of flight that the wheel would have filed as an exception. And in each case, the molar lines — consumer protection, regulatory requirements, statutory powers and duties — were not loosened but held more clearly, because they had been distinguished from everything else.

None of the three required exotic techniques. Each used tools that experienced practitioners already use informally: walking the floor, listening to the front line, trying things, consulting widely, keeping options open, following hunches. What the counter-design adds is a way of doing those things deliberately, naming them, connecting them and evidencing them — so that the practitioner's tacit wisdom becomes the organisation's explicit practice, and the shadow system's best moves are brought into the light without being frozen.

Coda — Still in the Middle

A rhizome has no conclusion. Deleuze and Guattari asked that their own conclusion be read last, and then built it as a set of definitions that send the reader back into the book. This coda does something similar. It gathers the argument into three movements — way, pattern, propensity — and ends where the book began: in the middle.

道 · Dào — the way that cannot be laid down in advance

The first line of the Dàodéjīng is usually translated as some variant of the way that can be spoken of is not the constant way. Whatever its original sense, the line has served for two and a half thousand years as a warning against mistaking a description of a path for the path. The wheel of 2015 is a description of a path. It is a good description, drawn with care by people who had walked many changes. But it presents itself as the way, and the way it presents is a circle that always returns to the same place.

The counter-design does not offer a better description of the same path. It offers a different relation to paths altogether: there are many, they cross, they are made by walking, and none of them returns to where it began. That is not a rejection of method. It is the recognition that in a complex world method is something one does, again and again, differently each time — clothed repetition, not bare — rather than something one has.

理 · Lǐ — the pattern in the grain

Lǐ originally named the veins in jade, the lines along which a skilled craftsman cuts so that the stone parts cleanly. It came to mean pattern, principle, the order immanent in things rather than imposed on them. The neo-Confucian philosophers made it the central term of their metaphysics: the world has a grain, and wisdom is following it.

The three lines of Part III are a theory of the grain of an organisation. Molar lines are the grain that the law and accountability have cut into it; they must be respected exactly. Molecular lines are the living grain of practice, which shifts as the organisation grows; they must be read afresh each time. Lines of flight are the places where the grain is changing — where a new pattern is forming that no existing category describes. A governance practice that knows these three kinds of grain can cut cleanly. One that treats the whole organisation as uniform material, to be shaped by design, will split the stone.

勢 · Shì — the propensity of things

Jullien's word for the strategic intelligence of classical China is efficacy, and his central term is shì: the potential carried by a configuration, the way a situation is already tending. A crossbow drawn, a boulder at the top of a slope, water gathered behind a dam. The skilled general does not fight the situation; he arranges it so that its own propensity carries the result, and acts at the moment when a small push is enough.

Every plateau of the counter-design is, in the end, a way of reading shì. Entering in the middle reads what is already moving. Composing the assemblage arranges the configuration. Reading the lines distinguishes what can be pushed from what must hold. Mapping shows the slope. Experimenting finds where a small push carries. Enunciating gathers the collective force. Territorialising stores the potential in a routine. Following the lines of flight watches for the moment when the situation's propensity changes.

The wheel, by contrast, belongs to the tradition Jullien sets against shì: the Western model of action as the imposition of a plan on a world that is assumed to be inert until acted upon. That model has built bridges and cathedrals. It has not, on the evidence of the past thirty years, governed financial institutions well.

Ten years on

The original's headline claimed that after ten years the methodology was still relevant. The counter-design's headline answers that after ten years we are still in the middle. Both are true. The wheel is still relevant, as a tool for the ordered domain and as a tracing that can be laid back on the map. And the people who use it are still in the middle — of changes that began before them, of regulatory regimes being rewritten as they comply, of organisations that no chart has ever captured.

What has changed in ten years is that the middle has become harder to ignore. The SM&CR asks senior managers to show reasonable steps in a world they cannot fully see. Operational resilience asks firms to plan for the failure they cannot prevent. The Consumer Duty asks for outcomes that no process can guarantee. Each of these is the regulatory system acknowledging, in its own vocabulary, that the firm is complex — and each sits awkwardly with a methodology that assumes it is not.

The counter-design is offered as one way to close that gap: a cartography rather than a methodology, a practice of reading lines rather than following stages, an assemblage of plateaus with no centre and many knots, and an evidence trail that shows not that a method was followed but that the organisation was paying attention. It will need to be adapted — no rhizome survives transplanting unchanged — and it will fail in the ways Chapter 13 describes unless it is held with care.

But its central claim is simple, and it can be carried out of the book on a single line.

GOVERN THE LINES, NOT THE STAGES. ENTER ANYWHERE. HOLD THE TREE WHERE IT IS OWED. FOLLOW WHAT ESCAPES. AND REMEMBER THAT YOU ARE ALWAYS, ALREADY, IN THE MIDDLE.

Glossary

Terms are defined in the sense used in this book. Deleuzian terms are given their working sense for governance, not a full philosophical exposition.

Adjacent possible
Stuart Kauffman's term for the set of states a system can reach in one step from its present state. It expands as the system moves, so the space of possible solutions grows as it is explored rather than existing in advance.
Agencement / assemblage
A working arrangement of heterogeneous elements — people, rules, systems, documents, moods — whose properties emerge from their interaction. Its parts keep their identity when detached (relations of exteriority), which is what makes recomposition possible.
Arborescence
The tree-like image of thought: a root, a trunk, branches; knowledge as genealogy, authority as hierarchy, information travelling along pre-established paths to a centre.
Asignifying rupture
The fourth principle of the rhizome: a break that is not immediately interpreted back into the structure it broke from, but is watched for what grows from it.
Black hole
Deleuze and Guattari's term for a line of flight that has turned destructive, or a territory that captures everything near it. In governance: fraud, rogue behaviour, a culture that swallows dissent.
Bricolage
Lévi-Strauss's term for working with what is to hand, repurposing it, as opposed to engineering from a plan with purpose-made parts. Most organisational change is bricolage.
Capacity
In DeLanda's assemblage theory, what a component can do in interaction with others, as opposed to its properties, which are what it is. Capacities are real but not given in advance.
Cartography
The fifth principle of the rhizome: making maps that are open, revisable and connectable, which intervene in what they map, rather than tracings that copy it.
Clothed repetition
Repetition that carries difference within it — a ritual performed anew, a principle produced afresh in each use — as opposed to bare repetition of the identical.
Collective assemblage of enunciation
The arrangement of institutions, practices and bodies that gives a statement its force. A policy has the force of the assemblage in which it is uttered.
Complex domain
In Snowden's Cynefin framework, the domain in which cause and effect can be perceived only in retrospect; the right approach is to probe, sense and respond.
Consent record
The counter-design's written form of an enunciation: what is agreed, by whom, on what conditions, for how long, with dissent recorded and renewal triggered by events.
Container / contained
Bion's terms for the capacity of a person or arrangement to take in unbearable feelings, hold them and return them in bearable form, enabling thought. Organisations need containers as much as controls.
Correction log
The record of molar breaches and their remediation, narrowed in scope so that it is not swamped by harmless variation.
Cynefin
A sense-making framework created by Dave Snowden, distinguishing clear, complicated, complex and chaotic domains, with confusion between them.
Decalcomania
Literally transfer-printing; for Deleuze and Guattari, tracing. The sixth principle of the rhizome asks that tracings always be put back on the map.
Deterritorialisation
The loosening of an assemblage's boundaries and the increase of its internal heterogeneity — the movement by which it comes apart or opens to the new.
Dissipative structure
Prigogine's term for an ordered pattern maintained by a continuous flow of energy and matter far from equilibrium, whose future form is selected at bifurcations by small fluctuations.
Drift into failure
Dekker's account of how complex systems slide towards catastrophe through a series of locally rational decisions, none of which is an error.
Heterarchy
McCulloch's term for an organisation in which different parts take the lead in different circumstances, with no fixed ranking.
Image of thought
Deleuze's term for the unexamined presuppositions about what it means to think — good will, recognition, representation, the primacy of results.
Line calibration
The counter-design's replacement for materiality assessment: the deliberate, recorded decision to move an element between molar and molecular lines.
Line of flight
A movement by which something escapes every existing segmentation. It can be creative or destructive. Posture: follow, don't suppress — and never endorse without judgement.
Line register
A register recording, for a service or process, which elements are molar (and why), which are molecular (and how much they vary), and who decided.
Mētis
James C. Scott's term for practical, local, experiential knowledge that resists codification and is often destroyed by attempts to make it legible.
Molar line
A rigid, segmented, binary line: law, regulation, accountability, limits. Posture: hold rigid. Danger: fear, which makes everything rigid.
Molecular line
A supple line of small, continuous changes in practice, interpretation and habit. Posture: keep supple, govern by attention. Danger: false clarity, micro-fascism.
Multiplicity
The third principle of the rhizome: a many that is not a plurality of some underlying one, and that changes in nature when divided.
n − 1
Deleuze and Guattari's instruction to subtract the unifying One from a multiplicity. Here, the version of a design that never closes and of a methodology that sits as one node among others.
Normalisation of deviance
Diane Vaughan's term, from her study of the Challenger launch decision, for the gradual acceptance of anomalies as normal, without anyone deciding it.
Plateau
From Bateson via Deleuze and Guattari: a region of sustained intensity not oriented towards a climax. In the counter-design, each of the eight activities that replace the wheel's stages.
Probe log
The record of safe-to-fail experiments: hypothesis, bounds, signals, review, outcome, and the decision to amplify, dampen or stop.
Refrain
Deleuze and Guattari's ritournelle: the repeated pattern by which a territory is made — a song in the dark, a home, a routine — always with a door left open.
Rhizome
A system without root or centre, in which any point can connect to any other, made of heterogeneous elements, which regrows when broken and is known by maps rather than tracings.
Safe-to-fail probe
A small, bounded experiment designed so that failure is tolerable and informative, run in a portfolio with others in the complex domain.
Safety-II
Hollnagel's view of safety as the presence of capacity to succeed under varying conditions, studied through what goes right, as against Safety-I's focus on failures.
Shì 勢
The propensity or potential carried by a configuration of forces; in Jullien's reading of Chinese strategy, the thing the skilled actor reads and works with.
Signal register
The record of lines of flight: what was noticed, how it was followed, the triage decision and the owner. A firm's peripheral vision.
SM&CR
The UK Senior Managers and Certification Regime, which allocates prescribed responsibilities to named individuals who must take reasonable steps to prevent breaches in their areas.
Territorialisation
The sharpening of an assemblage's boundaries and increase of its internal homogeneity — the movement by which it stabilises. Done lightly, it makes a refrain; done heavily, a prison.
Tracing
A representation that copies an existing structure and is checked against it. Useful, but dangerous when mistaken for the map.
Work-as-imagined / work-as-done
Hollnagel's distinction between work as described in procedures and work as actually performed, which adjusts continuously to conditions.
